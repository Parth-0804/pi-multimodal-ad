                                   Abstract
       Industrial monitoring increasingly relies on data from cameras, acoustic signals,
and machine sensors. Although multimodal learning can leverage the strengths of each
data type, adding sources does not necessarily improve prediction accuracy or reliability,
particularly when data quality or relevance varies.
       This thesis investigates the design and evaluation of process-aware multimodal
fusion architectures. It presents a critical review of 94 full-text works and five empirical
studies encompassing industrial gear monitoring, robotic assembly, milling,
manipulation, and human–robot handover. The research employs pretrained visual
representations, handcrafted sensor features, and patch-based temporal encoders. It
evaluates feature-level, prediction-level, and adaptive fusion, and also examines grouped
assessment and controlled input degradation where applicable.
       In the REASSEMBLE dataset, analysis of 4,530 completed actions from 148
recordings demonstrated that logistic stacking increased failure average precision from
0.429 for its sensor component to 0.447. However, the difference from equal averaging
(0.446) was small, and the advantage of stacking remained statistically unresolved.
Subsequent experiments indicated that training budget, feature aggregation, and
complementarity between representations can influence predictive performance.
External evaluations revealed that the benefits of fusion were not consistently observed,
and adaptive reweighting did not reliably enhance robustness.
       The results indicate that the value of multimodal fusion depends on the
prediction task, the available information, and the evaluation protocol, rather than
solely on architectural complexity. Issues such as independent welding validation,
predictive audio fusion, early warning, and generalization of unchanged models to new
domains remain unresolved. Consequently, the conclusions are limited to the tasks and
conditions examined in this study.
       Keywords: Multimodal learning, industrial monitoring, sensor fusion,
representation learning, adaptive fusion, robustness, cross-dataset evaluation

Introduction


1.1     Context and Motivation

Industrial monitoring means tracking the state and behavior of a physical process by
analyzing data collected during or after it runs. Industrial and robotic systems produce
many types of data, such as visual observations, acoustic signals, force measurements,
vibration, and other sensor signals (Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka et al., 2024b). Each data
type shows a different aspect of the process and may respond differently to changes in
operation.
       Multimodal learning enables the integration of these diverse data types,
particularly when each source provides complementary or unique information. However,
adding data sources does not inherently improve predictions (W. Wang et al., 2020).
The benefit depends on the information each modality retains, the relevance of the data
to the prediction task, and the methods used for data integration.
       Robotic welding was the starting point for this research. A camera can observe
the weld pool, while acoustic and machine sensor measurements give information about
other parts of the welding process. Previous studies have explored combining visual and
acoustic data for weld assessment (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026).
       The primary architectural challenge extends beyond welding. Industrial
monitoring systems require data representations that capture task-relevant information,



                                            2
Chapter 1. Introduction


fusion methods that effectively utilize complementary data from multiple sources, and
evaluation protocols that assess whether predictions remain reliable when some data are
missing or of low quality. Therefore, this research investigates multimodal fusion
architectures across a range of industrial and robotic monitoring tasks.



1.2     Problem Statement and Research Gap

The added value of additional modalities cannot be determined solely by the predictive
performance of the combined model; it is essential to compare the architecture’s
performance against credible unimodal predictors on identical prediction targets and
assessment observations.
       Prior work has shown that multimodal integration can improve prediction, but it
also identifies circumstances in which a jointly trained multimodal model will perform
worse than its unimodal counterparts (W. Wang et al., 2020). In one weld penetration
study, the image-only, audio-only, and fused models all achieved 100% accuracy on the
reported clean test set (Hua et al., 2026, Tables 1–3). There was therefore no
demonstrable benefit to the availability of an additional modality in terms of clean data
accuracy.
       Another challenge concerns the reliability of each data type. When an input is
missing, fallback mechanisms may be employed. If an input is present but degraded,
evidence is required to demonstrate altered reliability. While adaptive fusion can adjust
the contribution of each input, modifying these weights alone does not ensure improved
robustness (Ovadia et al., 2019; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).
       The fairness of these comparisons also depends on factors like the prediction
target, how the data is aligned over time, training methods, calibration, and whether
the test data is independent (Guo et al., 2017; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023; Ovadia
et al., 2019; Varma & Simon, 2006). Differences in any of these areas can make it hard
to know if performance gains are generalizable.
       This thesis addresses this gap by investigating the added predictive value and

robustness of multimodal fusion under fair, task-specific experimental conditions. It
investigates how the selection of data representation, complementary information, and
fusion method interact, and whether observed benefits in one context are transferable to
other industrial and robotic monitoring tasks.
       Chapter 2 reviews existing research to lay out the scientific background and
highlight the unresolved comparisons that define this research gap.



1.3     Research Questions and Objectives

The main goal of this thesis is to study how to design and evaluate process-aware
multimodal AI systems for reliable industrial monitoring and anomaly detection. It
examines how using specific data representations and different fusion methods affects
prediction, robustness, and practical usefulness.
       The first version of the exposé set out this main research question:


      How can a process-aware multimodal AI architecture be designed and
      evaluated for robust industrial anomaly detection using robotic welding data
      as a case study?


       Four subquestions address the main architectural and methodological aspects of
this study.


   SQ1. Which lightweight or pretrained Transformer-based representation components
         are suitable for visual, audio, and sensor time-series data in a multimodal
         industrial anomaly detection pipeline?

   SQ2. How do unimodal baselines, simple concatenation fusion, modular late fusion,
         and process-aware gated cross-modal fusion compare in terms of predictive
         performance?

   SQ3. Can a gated cross-modal fusion architecture improve robustness when one or
         more modalities are missing, degraded, or unreliable?

   SQ4. How can multimodal architecture performance be evaluated beyond accuracy,
         including robustness, interpretability, modularity, and practical usability?

       These considerations result in four research objectives: evaluating the suitability
of specific data representations, investigating various fusion strategies, testing
robustness to input degradation, and assessing the appropriateness of selected
evaluation methods for multimodal monitoring systems. Broader architectural questions
persist, despite changes in the original application scope.



1.4     Methodology Overview and Contributions

This research integrates a critical literature review with empirical investigations of
multimodal representation and fusion architectures. The review encompassed 94 full
texts, screened and assessed in two stages, with emphasis on architectural approaches,
unimodal baselines, fusion methods, evaluation protocols, and strategies for handling
unreliable inputs.
       Dataset selection also considered modality correspondence, annotation
suitability, assessment independence, and computational viability. The empirical
component compared modality-specific representations and prediction models, then
evaluated alternative fusion strategies and a series of controlled follow-up experiments.
       The implemented architectures use pre-trained convolutional visual
representations, statistical sensor features, and patch-based temporal encoders.
Information is combined using feature concatenation, probability averaging, logistic
stacking, and context-conditioned weighting.
       Evaluation covers predictive performance, calibration, grouped assessment,
uncertainty, and how the system behaves when inputs are missing or degraded,
depending on the dataset and protocol. Tests on external datasets check if the chosen
fusion methods and data representations work for different prediction tasks.
       The principal contributions of this thesis are:

   • Comparative evaluation of multimodal representations: This includes

      empirical tests of pretrained visual features, statistical sensor representations, and
      patch-based temporal encoders. The study compares training budgets, feature

  • Comparative evaluation of fusion architectures: Feature-level,

      the importance of target validity, data alignment, class-specific performance,

      datasets.

       The empirical work then shifted to related industrial and robotic monitoring
tasks, while keeping the original architectural research questions the same.
       PHM2026 focused on building condition targets and sensor-based prediction.
REASSEMBLE was the main experimental setting for comparing visual and sensor
data, fusion methods, and how systems handle degraded inputs. MSM, ViFailback, and

revealed the prediction labels, introducing a risk of label leakage. An acoustic
prediction branch was therefore not included.
       In this thesis, process-aware fusion means adjusting how predictions are
combined based on the process context and available quality information. Although the
registered title mentions physics-informed, the architectures do not use explicit
differential equations or conservation law constraints.
       The empirical work focuses on estimating conditions and classifying outcomes
within set observation periods. Classifying completed actions and manipulation
episodes does not, by itself, provide prospective anomaly detection or early warning.
       Robustness evaluation is limited to experiments where inputs were removed or
degraded in a controlled way. Studies with external datasets focus on re-using
architectures, fitting to specific datasets, and selected transfer methods, rather than
testing if an unchanged model works in a new domain.
       Therefore, the conclusions only apply to the architectures, prediction targets,
and evaluation conditions studied. Independent welding validation, predictive audio

Critical Literature Review


Multimodal Learning in Industrial and Robotic

Monitoring

Industrial monitoring covers tasks such as fault detection, checking a system’s physical
condition, assessing manufacturing quality, and confirming that a robot has completed
its task. The traditional approach uses single sensor readings and engineered features to
characterise the system’s behaviour. Learned representations take this further by
utilising data from images, sound and sensor sequences. Multimodal learning examines
how information from different modalities can be combined to support a common
prediction task (Baltrušaitis et al., 2019; Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka et al., 2024b).
       The literature touches on several related areas of research: traditional feature
engineering, the use of pre-trained representations, modelling over time, feature
combination, prediction fusion, and adaptive methods (where the method can adapt to
the context). They differ in the information they require, their computational cost, the
level of supervision they need, and their sensitivity to input quality.
       Industrial and robotic monitoring provides a platform on which to compare these
approaches as the process can be monitored via a number of different modalities
(cameras, acoustic sensors, machine sensors, etc.). Cameras will obviously provide a
view of the visible process, whereas the acoustic and machine sensors provide


                                             9
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


information which is not visible. The benefit of combining data depends heavily on
what each modality retains, the specific prediction you’re trying to make, and the
system’s test conditions (Goodarzi et al., 2023; Hua et al., 2026; W. Wang et al., 2020).
Simply increasing the amount of data does not necessarily lead to better predictions.
       Robotic welding is the principal driver of this thesis. Work on welding using
visual and acoustic data has shown that multimodal integration can improve prediction
performance, but also that, in some cases, adding a further modality provides no benefit
at all (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026). The Intel Welding audit documented prior
examination of the proposed assessment population, so an independent assessment of
weld quality was not possible on that population (see Chapter 1). The review therefore
addresses a more general architectural question across a range of industrial and robotic
monitoring tasks: how physical measurements, visual data, and sensor data can be
combined to assess system condition and task outcome.
       This chapter is organised around four research sub-questions: (SQ1) the
appropriateness of the representations, (SQ2) the incremental value of data integration,
(SQ3) reliability in the face of missing or degraded inputs and (SQ4) the need for
evaluation criteria that go beyond prediction accuracy. The aim is to present the
scientific evidence, identify the gaps in the comparisons and describe the experimental
design requirements. The architecture is detailed in Chapter 3 and the experimental
methodology in Chapter 4.



2.1     Review Scope and Search Methodology

The review examines industrial fusion claims through the methodologies, comparison
tables, and experimental results of the papers. It focuses on the link between the
proposed architecture, the available physical observations, the prediction target, and the
assessment protocol.
       A research gap is defined as a comparison that the existing body of scientific
evidence does not address for a given target, input set and assessment population. The



                                           10
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


ability to identify such a gap will, of course, depend on the search strategy and the
amount of published literature available.


2.1.1     Research Themes and Search Questions

The representation theme concerns what is being observed, what transformations are
being applied, and whether the representation retains the information relevant to the
task at hand. The fusion theme is about whether adding further inputs improves
performance on a standard prediction task compared with a set of plausible single-input
references.
        The robustness theme looks at which absence, corruption, and
distribution-change regimes were assessed. The evaluation theme looks at assessment
independence, thresholds, uncertainty, resource measurement and reproducibility.
        These themes intentionally include negative and inconclusive findings.
Comparisons where an additional modality yields minimal or no benefit can provide
valuable insights into representation suitability, redundancy, and the limitations of the
evaluated fusion procedure.
        The review clearly distinguishes between two objectives that are often confused.
Training with multiple modalities to deploy a single-input model is a privileged training
scenario. Combining multiple available inputs at deployment is a multimodal inference
problem. Cross-modal distillation and reconstruction link the two objectives but the
acquisition assumptions are very different.
        The taxonomy of Baltrušaitis et al. (2019) is the organising terminology. The
industrial claims are then assessed against the original studies rather than being entirely
inferred from that survey.

2.1.3      Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Direct industrial work focuses on faults, physical condition, manufacturing quality,
inspection, and the outcome of a robotic task. Dataset papers and legitimate unimodal
methods are also relevant, as fusion depends on a well-defined prediction target and a
suitable reference.

failure modes, missing-input mechanisms, uncertainty weighting, and evaluation theory
can all be valuable in designing industrial architectures, even if they do not amount to
industrial validation (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004; S. Lee et al., 2026; M. Ma et al., 2022;
W. Wang et al., 2020).

2.1.5     Limitations of the Review

The papers reviewed are by no means exhaustive and are not statistically independent.
Several inspection studies use the same MVTec 3D-AD or Eyecandies dataset, so
improvements on those benchmarks are not necessarily indicative of improvements on
independent industrial installations. The preprints are also version-specific, and some
publication details are still provisional.
        When relatively few studies examine a particular condition, the body of evidence
is such that any conclusions about that condition are necessarily weak.
        The main MVTec 3D-AD table gives 249 normal and 948 anomalous test
samples (total 1197) (Bergmann et al., 2022, Table 1). The welding penetration paper
reports inconsistent acquisition and training numbers, and the light inspection paper
reports different NwfDD values in its main table and its ablation table (Hua et al., 2026,
pp. 6708, 6711); (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, p. 6661, Tables IV, VI–VII).
        These inconsistencies reduce the precision of the comparisons. The studies
remain useful when their methods and reported findings are interpreted within clearly
stated limits.



2.2      Industrial Monitoring Tasks and Supervision

Different monitoring tasks rely on different assumptions about training data, available
observations, and acceptable levels of prediction error. These assumptions must be
understood prior to comparing representation learning and fusion architectures.
        The definition of the prediction target is absolutely crucial, as performance on
one industrial task cannot be considered valid evidence of performance on another.


2.2.1     Anomaly Detection, Defect Classification, and Failure

          Classification

Normal-only anomaly detection learns a reference for nominal observations. Supervised
defect classification, by contrast, uses labelled fault or quality classes. MVTec AD and

                                             14
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


MVTec 3D-AD are examples of the normal-only approach, whereas welding penetration
recognition is an example of supervised classification (Bergmann et al., 2019, 2022; Hua
et al., 2026).
        HANDOVER defines five completed-interaction outcome classes: success, no
approach, no grasp, no release and object drop (Thoduka et al., 2024b). A task failure
in the operational domain doesn’t necessarily mean that some physical damage has
occurred or that we can identify the root cause of that damage.
        Supervision is thus an important aspect of the evaluation. The weakly
supervised inspection study brings the MVTec data back in, as training requires
image-level anomalous labels, which of course changes the normal-only setting
(Z. Zhang et al., 2024, p. 6659).
        ZSG-IAD trains on reports and masks before evaluating on held-out categories
or datasets. The zero-shot terminology refers to that assessment boundary rather than
to the complete absence of anomaly supervised training (Q. Chen et al., 2026, PDF
pp. 6, 8–9).
        These differences establish the need to compare architectures against their
respective target definitions and under their respective supervision conditions.


2.2.2     Condition Estimation and Regression

Continuous prediction calls for attention to measurement units, reference observations,
and the horizon at which the target is defined. Remaining useful life is a very different
prediction problem from instantaneous defect probability.
        Al-Dulaimi et al. (2019) look at a hybrid CNN-LSTM on simulated C-MAPSS
trajectories using RMSE and an asymmetric prognostic score. Both branches of their
model process the same set of selected telemetry channels. The study therefore provides
evidence of complementary representations of the sensor data rather than any physical
camera/sensor complementarity.
        The synthetic robot benchmarks of Altinses and Schwung (2025) examine
cross-modal prediction, reconstruction, and downstream regression. They show the


                                            15
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


importance of relationships between representations, though errors on different
reconstructed outputs do not necessarily tell you which modality is more useful for a
given common prediction task.
        This issue is particularly relevant to proxy supervision. A model may accurately
predict a derived condition index, yet the relationship between that index and actual
physical deterioration may remain uncertain. A defensible regression comparison must
therefore define the target construction, measurement scale, fitted pre-processing, and
common assessment observations before interpreting numerical improvement as
indicative of enhanced condition monitoring.


2.2.3     Dataset-Provided Labels, Derived Targets, and Proxy

          Supervision

Reference labels have very different evidential roles. Post-build CT provides flaw
localisation for the voxel-level monitoring study by Petrich et al. (2021). Microscopy
and density assessments are used to define quality classes in J. Li et al. (2022).
Operando X-ray imaging is used to define process-state labels in Pandiyan et al. (2022).
        These studies show how an independent instrument can establish or validate a
prediction target without necessarily being available at the time of model inference. It
is vital to distinguish between supervision and predictive inputs when describing the
information available to a monitoring architecture.
        For derived labels, predictive performance must be distinguished from
independent physical validation. If the target is computed from the same measurements
supplied to the model, then a low prediction error may reflect the model recovering that
calculation rather than identifying an independent physical state.
        At the regional level, a single quality label may be assigned to many adjacent
observations. Their spatial or temporal dependence affects the choice of an appropriate
assessment unit.
        The empirical work in this thesis therefore distinguishes between native dataset
annotations, independent reference measurements, and analytical proxies. Each target

Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


type yields different conclusions regarding physical state and operational outcome.


2.2.4     Retrospective Assessment and Online Monitoring

The observation horizon is what determines when the model’s forecast becomes available.
A predictor assessed after the full observation period has elapsed is effectively given a
retrospective assessment unless its inputs are restricted to an earlier decision time.
        HANDOVER is very clear that its baselines classify outcomes post-trial, with
causal online prediction still being work in progress (Thoduka et al., 2024b, PDF p. 7
Section VI). A post-pass photo, a layer summary and the completed action all provide
different information horizons from prospective monitoring (Petrich et al., 2021;
X. Zhang et al., 2026).
        Retrospective classification is still useful for assessing representations and fusion
techniques, though its use in an operational context differs from that of an early
warning system.
        Computational delay must also be distinguished from observation time. DMAW
uses 20 frames at 37 fps and reports a much shorter forward-pass latency. As a result,
approximately half a second of observation is distinct from computational delay (Fan
et al., 2026, PDF pp. 6–7).
        Similarly, simply varying the input duration of a CNN–LSTM does not, by itself,
mean that a warning can be generated before a defect occurs (Pandiyan et al., 2022).
Active monitoring requires a defined information cut-off, acquisition delay and
end-to-end prediction workload.

2.3        Multimodal Data in Industrial Applications

Complementarity is determined by the additional task-relevant information provided by
an observation or representation, rather than by differences in tensor shape or the
number of acquisition sources.
        Different representations of the same physical quantity can contain
complementary information. Likewise, physically distinct sensors can provide redundant
or only very weakly relevant information for a given prediction target.
        The review therefore distinguishes between physical acquisition channels and
derived representations and considers ordinary pairing separately from cases requiring
stronger correspondence, such as cross-modal reconstruction or monitoring of a localised
process.

2.3.1     Visual and Video Observations

RGB images, thermal images, process videos and geometric observations all have
different acquisition assumptions. Depth, depth-derived point clouds, and estimated
surface normals can all be multiple views of the same geometric source.
        GPAD combines RGB-depth features with point cloud priors (M. Li et al., 2026,
PDF pp. 4–7). This is an example of integration across representations that do not
necessarily require three separate physical acquisition sources.
        RGB-geometry benchmarks describe appearance and shape rather than
controller dynamics or motor current (Bergmann et al., 2022; Bonfiglioli et al., 2023).
        Physical camera-plus-measurement precedents include thermal imaging with
vibration, welding images with sound, tool images with acoustic emission, and robot
video with force/torque and gripper state (Hua et al., 2026; Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka
et al., 2024b; X. Zhang et al., 2026).
        Their prediction units range from static inspection through to process windows
and full trials. In the tool wear study, the camera is used post-cutting pass, with the
preceding acoustic emission capturing the active cutting (X. Zhang et al., 2026, PDF
pp. 19–22).
        The representation of visual observations also influences the information
available. Both pooled frame features and video Transformers process multiple images,
but they differ in how they treat temporal relationships.
        These distinctions between them lead us to explicitly consider visual acquisition,
observation windows, and aggregation within multimodal monitoring architectures.


2.3.2     Sensor Time Series and Process Measurements

Machine measurements can vary enormously in their physical origin and in their
semantic meaning. An accelerometer measures vibration, a pressure sensor measures
fluid behaviour, and controller logs can contain commands, estimates, or tracking errors
rather than a direct measure of a physical quantity.
        The harmonic-drive study combines high-frequency vibration with servo

                                            19
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


information, whereas PGMTKD transfers pressure-vibration teacher knowledge to a
pressure-only student (C. Li et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024).
        Both papers address incomplete information, but their sensor availability and
deployment reasons differ.
        Representations can also inflate the apparent number of modalities without
including any new measurements. Kullu and Cinar (2022) combine current and
vibration using both raw and STFT representations. Wan et al. (2024) derive statistical
’vibration’ features from two acoustic sensors without acquiring an accelerometer
channel at all. C. Wang et al. (2025) combine source domain features with the decisions
from the vibration data.
        These studies are useful as architectural comparators, as they examine different
information representations. Their results should, of course, be treated as distinct from
comparisons that incorporate additional physical acquisition sources.
        An acquisition inventory and a description of the retained representations are
required prior to attributing any predictive gain to modality diversity.


2.3.3     Audio Observations

Audio can pick up machine/process events that are only very weakly visible. The
relationship to fault state depends heavily on background conditions, mic location, and
sync.
        MIMII deliberately incorporates factory background recordings at a range of
signal-to-noise ratios. Its fan baseline AUC drops from .94 at +6 dB to .70 at −6 dB
(Purohit et al., 2019, Table 3).
        ToyADMOS provides miniature machine recordings and an explicit noise-mixing
protocol, making it a useful reference for acoustic monitoring (Koizumi et al., 2019).
        In process monitoring, airborne acoustic signals can be paired with either a melt
pool camera or a photodiode. Signal-to-image encoding in the study by J. Li et al.
(2022) is an acoustic-optical-signal experiment, and Xie et al. (2025) use actual camera
images.

        DCASE2020 Task2 provides audio-only baselines under an evaluation protocol

2.3.4     Synchronisation, Correspondence, and Modality

          Availability

Fusion requires observations that refer to a compatible event, region or time interval.
Predicting one modality from another or combining aligned spatial discrepancy maps
requires stronger correspondence.
        RGB-point cloud feature mapping uses registered benchmark geometry. Process
monitoring may instead require time synchronisation and projection onto a common
spatial grid (Costanzino et al., 2024; Petrich et al., 2021).
        The digital twin study maps sensor features to robot TCP positions, showing
how position can establish correspondence even if it is not, in any normal sense, a
predictive feature (L. Chen et al., 2023).
        Modality availability has several levels. A file can exist without having a usable
paired observation for a given prediction unit. A file can have a successfully paired
observation which is still degraded.
        The acoustic-photodiode study explicitly lacks modality registration, which
limits claims about correspondence between its two encoded streams (J. Li et al., 2022).
        For camera and machine sensor monitoring, we can have an explicit pairing rule
that picks up unmatched events even if both underlying files are present. Modality
completeness should therefore be assessed on a prediction-unit basis rather than
inferred from the presence or absence of the files.

2.4      Representation Learning for Industrial

         Monitoring — SQ1

SQ1 concerns the relationship between representations and the requirements of an
industrial prediction task.
        The literature reviewed supports both engineered and learned representations.
Which is more appropriate will depend on the level of supervision, the level of
preprocessing, the level of independent sample support, the information required, and
the resources available for model fitting.
        This section sets out the scientific justification for considering these alternatives.
The details of their architecture and implementation are given in Chapter 3.


2.4.1     Engineered Features and Conventional Machine Learning

Engineered summaries impose a constraint on the information that a predictor can use.
Measures of location, spread, amplitude, and trend can describe an operating state
without requiring a high-capacity sequence model to be estimated from a small number
of independent observations.
        The literature is replete with evidence of compact representations. Hand-crafted
local geometry is competitive in 3D inspection, and a hydraulic CNN paper reports
1.7% error for its best single sensor versus 20.5% error for all seventeen sensors
(Goodarzi et al., 2023; Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023).
        These results highlight the necessity for robust conventional references and
careful selection of predictive information. The appropriateness of the specific
220-feature REASSEMBLE representation is established through task-specific
comparisons in Chapter 6, rather than assumed based on prior work.
        Industrial vision also offers lightweight alternatives. The weakly supervised
inspection network uses a single camera, internal anomaly-aware features, and a
compact CNN. Reported computational cost is 2.33 million parameters and 5.65 ms per
image (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, p. 6661, Table V).


                                              22
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


        The study provides some relevant evidence on representation eﬀiciency, but the
anomalous training labels and modified MVTec supervision differ significantly from
normal-only inspection.
        Conventional and learned representations should be compared using the same
prediction target, assessment observations, and appropriately fitted preprocessing,
rather than ranked solely by model family.


2.4.2     Pretrained Visual Representations

Pretrained visual representations provide a strong scientific explanation for transferring
knowledge learned from large image collections to industrial applications with far less
supervision. The features they learn may well contain the local appearance, texture,
and object configuration relevant to the downstream prediction task.
        PatchCore successfully employs pre-trained, locally aggregated intermediate
CNN features together with a memory of nominal patches for industrial inspection
(Roth et al., 2022, Section 3).
        This provides evidence for investigating transferred spatial representations,
though its nominal-only patch retrieval differs significantly from supervised action
classification.
        RT-DETR provides another architectural precedent in the form of its multi-scale
visual backbone (Y. Zhao et al., 2024, Sections 4.1–4.2). Features at several spatial
scales would be viable candidates for the prediction target.
        Its published detection accuracy and computational performance pertain to the
complete detection architecture rather than the downstream classification performance
of the extracted CNN features. Chapter 3 therefore distinguishes between the
components of the first-generation detector and those used in the thesis implementation.
        Asymmetric student-teacher and cross-modal mapping methods also provide
further evidence for task-specific use of the learned visual representations (Costanzino
et al., 2024; Rudolph et al., 2023).
        Vision-language inspection introduces semantic information as another potential


                                             23
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


source of representation value. DSE-AD combines historical and current rail images
with normal and abnormal position prompts and assesses pixel localisation on a further
rail domain (Lei & Qi, 2025).
        Its pair-reference and prompt assumptions differ significantly from those of
isolated image classification.
        Together, these studies point to pretrained visual features as plausible
candidates, while also highlighting the need to assess their actual information content,
trainable parameters, and observation horizon in the downstream monitoring task.


2.4.3     Temporal and Time-Series Representations

Temporal representation learning provides an opportunity to retain sequence
information that would otherwise be lost in engineered summaries.
        PatchTST addresses this through patch-based time-series representations and
shared modelling across channels (Nie et al., 2023, Sections 3.1–3.2).
        On the Weather dataset at a forecasting horizon of 96, the patch-plus-channel
independent model obtains an MSE of .152, compared with .164 without patching (Nie
et al., 2023, Table 7).
        This provides positive evidence for patch-based representation learning in the
forecasting setting under consideration. However, the relevance to industrial
classification warrants separate investigation, as the prediction target, normalisation,
supervision, and aggregation differ.
        The exact formulation of PatchTST and its adaptation to the thesis experiments
are given in Chapter 3.
        Video representation learning looks at another temporal structure. TimeSformer
and Video Swin model the spatial and temporal relations between video frames
(Bertasius et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2022).
        These approaches provide an alternative to simply averaging independently
encoded frames. The industrial CNN-LSTM remaining useful life study also shows the
importance of observation length within its simulated prediction task (Al-Dulaimi et al.,


                                              24
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


2019).
         The literature therefore motivates comparisons that explicitly account for
sequence length, observation windows, temporal ordering, normalisation, pre-training,
and fitting budget.
         A Temporal Transformer and a compact statistical representation could well be
preserving different aspects of the same sensor stream. Looking at their individual and
combined performance helps assess whether the additional representation contains
useful information for the task at hand.


2.4.4      Audio Representations

Acoustic representations include MFCCs, log-mel spectra, wavelet maps, GASF
transforms and learned features.
         The Welding Penetration Network uses VMD and GASF; DMAW uses
Transformer representations adapted to the welding observations (Fan et al., 2026; Hua
et al., 2026).
         These are alternative forms of acoustic representation. An image-shaped acoustic
representation is still one derived from sound rather than being an independently
acquired camera stream.
         The Audio Spectrogram Transformer is a pretrained candidate for acoustic
representation learning (Y. Gong et al., 2021). The results on the general audio
benchmark are suﬀiciently promising to warrant industrial investigation. However, the
appropriateness of such representations will depend heavily on the particular acoustic
environment and the specific prediction task.
         MIMII and ToyADMOS both go some way to establishing the importance of
background composition and noise environment (Koizumi et al., 2019; Purohit et al.,
2019).
         Together, these studies lead one to consider recording quality, normalisation, and
consistent observation intervals when looking at audio representations. The
REASSEMBLE correspondence checks precluded the corresponding predictive

2.4.5     Frozen Representations, Fine-Tuning, and

          Computational Restrictions

Freezing an encoder, fitting a small prediction head and then adapting the entire
network all consume different amounts of statistical and computing resources.
        Benchmark methods based on pretrained features show that you can get useful
representations without going through the entire original representation-learning
process on the industrial dataset (Costanzino et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2022).
        The computational cost of pretraining is still part of the representation’s
provenance even if it’s external to the downstream experiment.
        Frozen feature extraction can therefore enable comparisons under a constrained
fitting budget. Fine-tuning provides a very different opportunity in that it allows you to
adapt the representations to the particular target observations. Which of the two is
ultimately more effective can only be determined by empirical comparison, rather than
by assuming one approach is in principle better than the other.
        Distillation also makes a further distinction between training and deployment.
The PGMTKD multimodal teachers are used to enrich a pressure-only student. The
gated acoustic-vibration study reports a distilled LightGBM student, though the nature
of the teacher features and the timing of the inputs remain ambiguous (Huang & Tong,
2026; C. Li et al., 2025).
        Resource comparisons should therefore account for feature extraction,
preprocessing, trainable components, and any teachers required during inference, rather
than just the final prediction model.
        For SQ1, these results are suﬀicient to justify making explicit comparisons of the
representation and fitting procedures under plausible computing budgets.

2.5      Multimodal Fusion Strategies — SQ2

Fusion strategies can be broken down according to where the information is fused. Early
integration fuses raw observations or raw features. Intermediate integration fuses the
learned representations. Prediction-level fusion fuses the outputs (e.g., class probability
estimates, anomaly scores). Hybrid architectures use more than one level of integration.
        These provide an operational taxonomy for comparing the studies reviewed.
Adaptive reweighting refers to whether the combination varies according to the
observation or its context and can therefore operate at different fusion levels.
        Terminology from the source papers is kept where appropriate, and comparisons
are based on the actual location and mechanism of information exchange.


2.5.1     Unimodal References and Incremental Modality Value

The strongest credible single-input predictor serves as the benchmark for investigating
SQ2.
        The original welding motivation is why this reference is so important. DMAW
fuses the image and audio Transformer representations to classify the four welding
states. The reported accuracy is 96.1% with fusion, 90.2% for the image model and
84.4% for the audio model (Fan et al., 2026, PDF p. 7, Table III).
        This supports a predictive benefit from the assessed multimodal pipeline. The
grouping of the assessment windows by welding run is still not resolved in the
evaluation.
        In contrast, the weld penetration study reports 100% clean test accuracy for its
image-only, audio-only, and fused models (Hua et al., 2026, Tables 1–3).
        The additional modality therefore provides no measurable gain in accuracy
under that comparison.
        A similar distinction is made in the case of robotic monitoring. The
HANDOVER study reports 69.1% accuracy for video plus force/torque and 67.9% when
gripper state is added to the I3D-D family (Thoduka et al., 2024b, PDF p. 7, Table III).



                                            27
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


        These are five-class outcome accuracies averaged over five training runs, rather
than binary failure average precision. The comparison does, however, show that adding
an observation can reduce predictive performance.
        The tool wear study reports an MAE of 6.6183 for their proposed model, 8.3002
for the visual ResNet50 and 60.5118 for the acoustic reference. All the other
multimodal architectures evaluated have errors in excess of 45 (X. Zhang et al., 2026,
PDF p. 24, Table 1).
        The strong visual reference makes the improvement scientifically informative,
though the architectural differences mean you cannot attribute all of it to fusion.
        Transferable results on the diﬀiculties of multimodal optimisation and on
industrial all-channel losses underline the need to establish incremental gains against a
strong admissible reference (Goodarzi et al., 2023; W. Wang et al., 2020).
        Collectively, these studies support investigating the value of conditional fusion,
rather than assuming that including additional modalities will necessarily improve
prediction.


2.5.2       Feature Concatenation

Feature concatenation combines the representations with no explicit mechanism for
adaptive allocation and is therefore a key reference point for any more sophisticated
form of integration.
        Effectiveness depends on feature scale, dimensionality, regularisation, and the
quality of the constituent representations.
        M3DM’s direct concatenation ablation is worse than its point cloud reference,
while the full hybrid system outperforms both individual inputs (Y. Wang et al., 2023,
Table 4).
        MADFlow also provides a further protocol-specific comparison between
alignment and concatenation (Y. Li et al., 2025).
        CFA provides transferable evidence from forecasting. For DLinear on the Energy
dataset, the first concatenation gives an MSE of .670, compared with .263 without text


                                              28
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


and .243 with constrained fusion (S. Lee et al., 2026, PDF p. 4, Table 4).
        The constrained fusion method also performs worse in a number of the evaluated
scenarios, which again suggests that the benefits of an integration mechanism are highly
task-dependent.
        Manufacturing fusion can similarly improve one architecture at the expense of
the other under the same broad prediction task (J. Li et al., 2022, Table 3).
        These results establish concatenation as a valid comparator. It should be
appropriately normalised and fitted, rather than implemented as an artificially weak
baseline against which more complex methods are compared.


2.5.3     Prediction-Level and Late Fusion

Prediction-level fusion involves fusing the outputs of modality-specific predictors via
techniques such as averaging, stacking, or evidence aggregation. This is a classification
of where the fusion occurs; the individual predictors could be trained independently or
as part of a wider training regime.
        Thermal-vibration diagnosis provides direct evidence of physical camera-sensor
prediction-level fusion. They use separate CNN softmax outputs with weighted
Dempster-Shafer aggregation and report a clean 100% diagnostic rate, compared with
99.06% for IRT and 96.32% for vibration (Huo et al., 2022, Table VII).
        The weights were fixed, and the sample-level cross-validation did not establish
independence between physical runs.
        HANDOVER compares late outputs with intermediate sensor integration in the
RGB and optical flow branches (Thoduka et al., 2024b, PDF pp. 5–7).
        Cross-modal feature mapping and CPIR combine discrepancy maps, which need
not be probabilities (Costanzino et al., 2024; Shangguan et al., 2025).
        Some papers also use the term ’late fusion’ to describe representation-level
integration prior to classification (W. Wang et al., 2020). That underlines the
importance of knowing what information you are actually fusing.
        Stacked generalisation provides a scientific basis for learning a second-stage


                                             29
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


combination from cross-validated predictions. Probability outputs contain predictive
information beyond the hard class labels so that a second-stage learner can estimate the
joint utility of the outputs (Ting & Witten, 1999, Sections 2–3).
        The referenced study looks at a linear least-squares combiner; this thesis looks at
logistic stacking. F2 is low-dimensional because it combines two scalar branch
probabilities. Its suitability still requires task-specific evaluation.
        The construction of grouped prediction banks, calibration, and the common
outer assessment remain key to evaluating the learned combination. The exact form of
the F2 and the fitting procedure are given in Chapters 3 and 4 respectively.


2.5.4     Gated and Context-Conditioned Fusion

Adaptive fusion is scientifically justified when the utility of the individual inputs varies
from observation to observation.
        Gated Multimodal Units do provide evidence of transferable conditional
representation integration. The architecture fuses visual and textual information and
achieves a macro F-score of .541, compared with .521 for the concatenation baseline in
the movie-genre classification task (Arevalo et al., 2017, Section 3.1, Table 2).
        This finding points to the need to look at conditional allocation, though the
mechanism at the application and representation level is quite different from the
probability- and logit-based gates that have been the subject of this thesis.
        Adaptive integration has also been looked at in an industrial context. DMAW
achieved 96.1% accuracy, compared with 90.5% for summation and 92.6% for its gate
comparator (Fan et al., 2026, PDF p. 9, Table V).
        TC-MAF provides another approach whereby the auxiliary RGB evidence is
bounded around a multimodal anchor using normal training dispersion and local
disagreement (Deng et al., 2026, PDF pp. 3–5).
        These architectures illustrate various approaches to controlling information
contribution.
        An important evaluation dimension is what governs the allocation. GCCF-AD is


                                              30
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


described as dynamic fusion, but the formulation they report is a learned scalar with no
explicit sample-quality input (An et al., 2026, p. 149226, Eq. 8).
        PGMTKD uses training losses to assign teacher contributions, and task loss
balancing focuses on optimisation rather than any direct measure of input quality at
deployment (C. Li et al., 2025; X. Zhang et al., 2026).
        GAF-Net uses progressive feature feedback, and trustworthy fusion employs
evidence-based aggregation (Huang & Tong, 2026; Ying et al., 2025).
        The scientific distinction is what contributions can vary, what information
determines that variation, and whether that variation improves predictive performance
relative to a fixed combination.
        Process-conditioned weighting provides an additional way to incorporate
pertinent context, without requiring explicit physical law-constrained modelling.


2.5.5     Comparability and Fairness of Fusion Evaluations

A shared dataset name does not in itself constitute a matched experimental comparison.
        Cohort construction, supervision, observation windows, pretraining, feature
dimensionality, augmentation, and selection exposure could all contribute to differences
between architectures.
        The tool wear study alters the architectural components as part of the fusion,
and the incomplete federated imagery study alters the client data access as well as the
combining mechanism (X. Zhang et al., 2026; X. Zhao et al., 2026).
        These are comparative assessments of fitted pipelines. Their interpretation
differs from an experiment intended to isolate attention, modality contribution, or
quality conditioning.
        Comparisons using the same evidence sources can support a narrower
attribution. TC-MAF retains the same evidence sources, comparing bounded fusion
against static averaging, weighted scores, rank fusion, and a learned gate (Deng et al.,
2026, PDF p. 7, Table 3).
        On the flip side, the disagreement-aware study uses a locally released code


                                            31
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


baseline that performs worse than the published M3DM result (J. Xu et al., 2026, PDF
p. 10, Table 4).
        Distilled predictors can also access training information that is not available to
standard unimodal models (C. Li et al., 2025).
        These settings remain scientifically valuable, provided that the information and
fitting conditions are clearly specified. Pipeline performance, incremental modality
value, and the isolated effect of a fusion operator represent distinct evaluation questions.



2.6      Reliability and Robustness — SQ3

The reliability of a multimodal architecture depends on both the prediction setting and
the type of intervention acting on its inputs.
        Missing channels, corrupted measurements, asynchronous observations and
changes in operating conditions all represent different reliability issues. The evidence
reviewed is therefore grouped by the particular condition under consideration rather
than assigned to a single general robustness category.


2.6.1     Missing Modalities

Planned single-input deployment differs from an unannounced loss of an input in a
model that normally expects complete observations.
        PGMTKD’s pressure-only student and incomplete RGB-geometry methods do
provide evidence for reduced input inference. ModDrop and the missing-modality
Transformers add further transferable mechanisms and counterexamples (C. Li et al.,
2025; M. Ma et al., 2022; Neverova et al., 2015; Sui et al., 2026).
        The effectiveness of these mechanisms will, of course, depend on the training
conditions and the specific missing-input patterns they have been assessed on.
        TC-MAF explicitly evaluates unavailable 3D input. I-AUROC drops from .9788
to .9330 and P-AUPRO from .9901 to .9586 (Deng et al., 2026, PDF p. 7, Table 3).
        This provides a quantified example of performance under a given missing


                                             32
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


modality condition.
        Federated substation monitoring varies the proportion of incomplete clients to
80% (X. Zhao et al., 2026, Figure 8).
        Its training and client-availability setting differs from an arbitrary inference-time
camera failure.
        ZSG-IAD reports input subset comparisons without fully defining the shared
checkpoint and training exposure (Q. Chen et al., 2026). Missing training images in the
hot rolling regime constitute another availability regime (L. Ma et al., 2025).
        These studies collectively indicate the need to explicitly specify available
modalities during both training and inference (i.e., availability masks), the policy for
replacement or fallback, and the system’s response when all required inputs are absent.


2.6.2     Noise and Partial Degradation

Physical monitoring studies do provide evidence of a number of controlled degradation
mechanisms. Thermal-vibration diagnosis examines Gaussian noise and vibration
bias/stuck faults, while welding penetration recognition examines artificial interference.
Acoustic-thermal and acoustic-vibration studies look at other noise settings (Hua et al.,
2026; Huang & Tong, 2026; Huo et al., 2022; Ying et al., 2025).
        These interventions vary in the observations affected, the degree of corruption
and the extent to which the model is exposed to degradation.
        At −2 dB, noisy IR thermography gives 95.26%, which jumps to 97.6% when
combined with fault-free vibration. Noisy vibration alone gives 36.86%, jumping to
94.2% when combined with fault-free IR thermography (Huo et al., 2022, Table VIII).
        The comparison shows the value of a fault-free companion modality when
another observation is synthetically corrupted.
        The welding penetration study reports a fused accuracy of 93.33% under its
interference condition (Hua et al., 2026, Table 3).
        The corresponding corrupted unimodal comparisons are required in order to
assess the incremental benefit of fusion in the interference condition. The reported


                                             33
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


intervention also leaves some uncertainty about the corruption operator, magnitude,
and exposure matching.
        Synthetic signal-to-noise interventions provide some controlled experimental
evidence, but the conclusions are specific to the observations affected and the severities
tested. Natural simultaneous degradation and ordinary defective objects are quite
different phenomena.
        These distinctions lead directly to a comparison of predictions from degraded
input against those from static fusion and single-input predictors under the same
reliability condition.


2.6.3     Temporal Misalignment and Distribution Shift

Temporal misalignment and distribution shift both affect multimodal prediction, albeit
in different ways.
        A clock offset can break correspondence between observations, whereas a
distribution shift can change the conditions under which the learned representation or
predictor is evaluated.
        Registration studies have established the importance of alignment in multimodal
monitoring. OmniFuser’s repeated within-pass images provide a way to construct paired
observations rather than directly test a synchronisation failure (L. Chen et al., 2023;
Petrich et al., 2021; Z. Wang et al., 2025).
        The literature also contains examples of evaluation under changed acquisition
conditions. HANDOVER trains on one robot and evaluates on a different robot, and
there’s a rail localisation study that evaluates a Subway-trained model on high-speed
rail (Thoduka et al., 2024b, PDF p. 7, Table V); (Lei & Qi, 2025, PDF p. 10, Table III).
        Target-adaptive methods, target-validated condition studies and independent
build experiments are all different forms of access to the assessment domain (Bevans
et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2024; Huang & Tong, 2026; C. Wang et al., 2025).
        Rebuilding normal memories on Eyecandies using fixed fusion hyperparameters
is evidence of recipe transfer, rather than deploying an unaltered model to an


                                               34
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


untouched target installation (Deng et al., 2026; J. Xu et al., 2026).
        These distinctions between them are important for interpreting the external
dataset work in this thesis. Architecture reuse, representation transfer, temporal offset
tolerance, and unchanged model generalisation each require distinct evaluation
protocols.


2.6.4     Quality-Aware and Uncertainty-Aware Fusion

Quality-aware fusion is driven by the idea that the predictive utility of an input may
vary with its quality. Signal quality, model confidence, calibrated probability and
evidence consistency are all different beasts, though.
        QMF furnishes a theoretical basis for studying sample-dependent weighting via
generalisation upper bounds under stated relationships between uncertainty and loss,
assuming a common training and test distribution (Q. Zhang et al., 2023,
Sections 3.1–3.2).
        The transferable UDML experiments train on controlled Gaussian corruption
intensities and recognise that sample diﬀiculty can be confused with modality
corruption (Wei et al., 2026, PDF pp. 4–5, 9).
        These results lead us to ask whether the quality information we have available
helps us identify when a particular observation is no longer useful.
        QMF reports an NYU scene classification accuracy of 70.09% on clean inputs,
compared to 70.30% for concatenation. At corruption intensity ten, it reports 55.60%
compared to 51.99% for late fusion (Q. Zhang et al., 2023, PDF p. 8, Table 1).
        These non-industrial RGB-depth comparisons do show a regime-specific
advantage under corruption. The clean and corrupted comparisons quoted use different
reference methods, so they should not be read as a clean-versus-corrupted trade-off
against the same reference method.
        The effect of the adaptation mechanisms also differs across prediction endpoints.
The disagreement add-on increases the local MVTec image AUROC from .779 to .800
and, at the same time, reduces the Eyecandies image AUROC from .810 to .794, even


                                            35
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


though the pixel metrics have improved (J. Xu et al., 2026, PDF pp. 10–11, Tables 4–5).
        TC-MAF uses normal-training dispersion and local disagreement to control the
contribution of auxiliary evidence (Deng et al., 2026).
        Collectively, these findings establish a scientific basis for reducing reliance on a
stream when the observable context is expected to indicate errors. They also underscore
the need to assess the relationship between available quality indicators and actual
predictive reliability.
        QMF’s theoretical justification does not validate the use of arbitrary quality
proxies, and clean calibration can actually degrade after a distribution shift (Ovadia
et al., 2019; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).
        For the empirical investigation, the relevant comparison is whether
quality-conditioned weighting improves performance over a static model with the same
level of corruption exposure, while still maintaining an acceptable level of clean
performance.


2.6.5     Modality Dropout, Augmentation, and Fallback

          Mechanisms

Different robustness mechanisms deal with different aspects of input availability.
        Training-time modality dropout exposes the model to certain absence patterns.
Imputation produces substitute observations, and deterministic fallbacks route the
prediction to an available unimodal component.
        These approaches can generate similar outputs under certain conditions without
relying on the same learned recovery mechanism.
        Fuzzy regularisation works by introducing failures via partial corruption and by
replacing the entire input with a constant or noise. The evaluation is of Gazebo pose
regression using random sample splits of overlapping windows (Altinses & Schwung,
2023, PDF pp. 3–5).
        The investigation provides evidence for controlled input regularisation, with
independent physical deployment outage performance remaining unaddressed.

                                              36
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


        OmniFuser describes dropout and masking without any quantified outage table
(Z. Wang et al., 2025, PDF p. 10).
        Generated replacement observations can also have a negative impact on
performance. Replacing the clean depth with CycleGAN output worsens the synthetic
robot benchmark’s MSE (from 2.1745 × 10−3 to 13.472 × 10−3 ) (Altinses & Schwung,
2025, Table 7).
        These results motivate individual evaluation of learned recovery, training
exposure, and explicit fallback policies. Static controls, with equivalent degradation
exposure and predictions from the surviving input, provide a useful baseline for
assessing adaptive robustness.



2.7       Evaluation Beyond Accuracy — SQ4

The practical value of a monitoring architecture will depend entirely on how its
predictions are evaluated, chosen, and interpreted.
        Accuracy in prediction can be affected by class distribution, assessment grouping
and decision thresholds. A single aggregate score therefore only provides one facet of
the evidence needed to assess the performance of a multimodal industrial monitoring
system.
        SQ4 looks at assessment independence, discrimination, calibration, uncertainty,
interpretability, computational viability and reproducibility.


2.7.1     Grouped Evaluation and Leakage Prevention

The independent assessment unit may be a weld, a tool, a participant, a recording, a
machine or a build.
        Randomly partitioned observations can end up with shared acquisition
contextual information or raw samples. The fuzzy-regularisation study forms 20-step
windows with a stride of one and then randomly divides the resulting examples
(Altinses & Schwung, 2023, PDF pp. 4–5).


                                            37
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


        Its assessment therefore doesn’t establish performance on unseen trajectories.
        Weld run grouping is still unresolved in the closest welding studies (Fan et al.,
2026; Hua et al., 2026). These examples do show a potential dependence between the
observations but do not establish any particular level of leakage.
        The HANDOVER authors report an explicit participant-disjoint oﬀicial split.
An adapted learned fusion implementation would still require participant information in
order to build the corresponding inner partitions (Thoduka et al., 2024b).
        Normalisation, feature selection and model parameter optimisation all require an
appropriate split between model development and model assessment (Kapoor &
Narayanan, 2023; Varma & Simon, 2006).
        Out-of-fold stacking provides a mechanism for constructing second-stage
prediction inputs without any in-sample predictions. Its validity is of course also
dependent on the way in which the underlying fitting, selection and calibration folds are
constructed.
        Repeatedly selecting follow-up experiments after looking at the assessment
results introduces further selection bias, even if the individual model fits would have
ruled out their outer test labels.
        These results motivate the development of assessment protocols that account for
the physical origin and dependence of observations throughout the modelling process.


2.7.2     Discrimination, Calibration, and Decision Thresholds

Ranking, probability quality and thresholded decisions are three different aspects of a
predictor.
        AUROC measures discrimination across thresholds rather than performance at a
single operational false alarm rate or the quality of the predicted probabilities.
        MVTec AD shows that useful ranking can exist alongside fairly poor thresholded
localisation. The strict FPR metrics highlight a different aspect of detection
performance (Bergmann et al., 2019; Cheng et al., 2025; Costanzino et al., 2024).
        The rail vision-language study reports pixel-level metrics and optimal threshold


                                            38

F1 (Lei & Qi, 2025, PDF p. 7). These metrics address a completely different evaluation
problem than image-level alarm accuracy at a threshold chosen from the training or
validation set.
        In manufacturing, Petrich et al. (2021) report an accuracy of .9921 and a
precision of .2487 on an imbalanced layer, which is a clear example of the impact of
class distribution and threshold selection.
        Validation-fitted calibration can improve in-distribution probability quality, while
dataset shift can damage confidence estimates (Guo et al., 2017; Ovadia et al., 2019).
        Proper scoring rules (negative log-likelihood, Brier score, etc.) provide a basis for
assessing probability forecasts (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). Their interpretation differs
from that of discrimination or thresholded classification.
        A generated confidence field or evidence mass cannot replace probability
evaluation. Metrics should align with the prediction target and class distribution, and
operational thresholds should be selected using admissible training or validation
observations.


2.7.3     Uncertainty and Statistical Comparisons

Seed variation, acquisition group sampling and model selection uncertainty are all
different sources of variation.
        HANDOVER’s reported standard deviations are for five training runs on a fixed
test set, whereas a group bootstrap of saved predictions samples assessment units
conditional on the fitted models (Thoduka et al., 2024b).
        These approaches capture different facets of uncertainty and do not necessarily
encompass all the variation expected from retraining or deployment in a different
domain.
        Cross-validation folds share training observations. Under the assumptions stated,
the classic variance result shows that no universally unbiased estimator of
cross-validation variance exists; ignoring covariance can substantially underestimate
uncertainty (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004, pp. 1096–1098, 1102–1104). Conditional


                                              39
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


uncertainty intervals can still be useful when the estimand and dependence assumptions
are stated explicitly.
        Small fusion effects, ambiguous repetition counts and conflicting descriptions of
significance all warrant the same level of caution (Gu et al., 2024; J. Xu et al., 2025).
        Paired performance differences on common assessment observations provide a
much more direct comparison than unrelated score intervals. An interval which contains
zero doesn’t demonstrate equivalence, and a positive point estimate doesn’t establish
practical importance without an appropriate criterion.


2.7.4     Interpretability and Its Limits

Prediction scores, coeﬀicients, gate traces and spatial maps can all tell you something
regarding how the model is behaving.
        The attention debate has both counterexamples to faithful explanation and
evidence that the learned attention weights do, in fact, contain useful information. This
leads to the conclusion that interpretability should be evaluated on a task-specific basis
rather than drawing general conclusions from the terminology of the architecture itself
(Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019).
        ZSG-IAD measures schema validity, language consistency and
coarse-to-final-mask overlap (Q. Chen et al., 2026). These provide evidence of various
forms of self-consistency.
        Their interpretation differs from both independent physical defect causation and
calibrated confidence.
        HANDOVER leaves the use of human action prediction as a means of
explanation as a future area of research (Thoduka et al., 2024b).
        Within this thesis, observable quality responses and inspectable fusion
coeﬀicients provide descriptive transparency. However, expert understanding, causal
attribution, and operator usability require additional assessments.
        Process context should be distinguished from the explicit physical equation
constraints (e.g. PGMTKD’s pressure model (C. Li et al., 2025)).

2.7.5     Modularity, Reproducibility, and Computational Cost

Modular architectures enable you to swap in and out individual representations and
prediction components and compare them. Their value for scientific investigation comes
from the fact that you can identify which component is different between conditions.
        Modularity does not establish predictive robustness or an advantage over a
matched end-to-end architecture.
        An identified version, accessible source code, executable processing and
independently reproduced results all provide various levels of reproducibility assurance
(Pineau et al., 2021).
        The difference between local and published M3DM results remains unresolved. It
motivates checks of dependencies, valid point filtering, checkpoints, and scoring
conventions (J. Xu et al., 2026).
        Computational comparisons of course also require a common measurement
boundary. The CPIR paper reports lower FPS than CFM, which is a clear performance
vs speed trade-off (Shangguan et al., 2025).
        The reported 5.65 ms for the lightweight image network doesn’t include the
independent sensor branch or acquisition delay (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, p. 6661, Table V).
        TimeSformer’s compute requirements depend on the ImageNet pretraining and
the workload it is evaluated on (Bertasius et al., 2021).
        Hardware, batch size, caching, synchronisation and the processing stages
included all determine which computational measurements can be compared.
        These results indicate the necessity for explicit resource accounting and
reproducibility documentation. Prospective deployment and operator usability remain
open questions for validating a successful offline forward pass.



2.8      Critical Synthesis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature shows that multimodal observations and learned representations
can benefit industrial and robotic monitoring. Their value, however, depends on the


                                            41
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


prediction task, the information each representation contains, and the conditions under
which the integration method is evaluated.
         The previous sections have identified four related scientific questions: which
representations are appropriate for a given target, whether combining their predictions
is valuable, whether that value is maintained as observation quality degrades, and how
the experimental assessments should be conducted.
         This section synthesises existing findings and outstanding scientific questions to
establish design requirements for the empirical work.


2.8.1      Findings Supported Across the Reviewed Studies

There are tangible observations that can be used to improve the performance of a
task-specific monitoring pipeline. DMAW, thermal-vibration diagnosis, HANDOVER
and tool wear prediction are all good examples under their respective assessment
protocols (Fan et al., 2026; Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka et al., 2024b; X. Zhang et al.,
2026).
         Controlled evidence integration can also improve RGB–geometry inspection
under intact-input conditions. TC-MAF separately reports performance when the 3D
input is unavailable (Deng et al., 2026).
         The literature therefore establishes that useful industrial multimodal fusion
mechanisms and approaches to dealing with incomplete observations do in fact exist.
         Representation learning offers a number of scientifically sound options for
extracting information relevant to the task. Pretrained convolutional features,
patch-based temporal representations, and hand-crafted features are all candidates with
some supporting evidence under certain learning and evaluation regimes (Horwitz &
Hoshen, 2023; Nie et al., 2023; Roth et al., 2022).
         Their suitability for another industrial prediction target will, of course, depend
on several factors, including the observations, the supervision, the aggregation, and the
compute resources involved.
         Static and learned fusion mechanisms both offer different avenues for exploiting


                                              42
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


complementary information. Stacked generalisation motivates learning combinations of
the outputs of individual predictors. In contrast, gated representation learning and
quality-aware fusion justify context-dependent allocation (Arevalo et al., 2017; Ting &
Witten, 1999; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).
        A further well-established distinction concerns input reliability. Planned
single-input deployment, complete inference-time absence, controlled corruption, and
target domain adaptation all address different issues (Altinses & Schwung, 2023; C. Li
et al., 2025; X. Zhao et al., 2026).
        The consistent finding across the reviewed studies is a conditional benefit under
specific tasks and evaluation regimes, rather than universal superiority of any single
representation or fusion architecture.


2.8.2       Unresolved Questions and Research Gap

The evidence reviewed also contains significant negative and, indeed, conflicting
findings.
        The same clean-test accuracy is observed for the unimodal and fused welding
models. HANDOVER performance drops when the gripper state is added. Some
alternative multimodal tool-wear models perform worse than the visual reference,
although the proposed model improves on it. The Eyecandies image scores also go
down. All of this calls into question the notion of an unqualified fusion benefit (Hua
et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b; J. Xu et al., 2026; X. Zhang et al., 2026).
        Category-specific geometry losses, poor hydraulic channel combinations, and the
diﬀiculties of multimodal optimisation all reinforce this conclusion (Bonfiglioli et al.,
2023; Goodarzi et al., 2023; Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023; W. Wang et al., 2020).
        Several mechanisms could account for these results: redundancy, inappropriate
representations, optimisation diﬀiculties, noise, supervision and limited independent
sample support. The studies do not point to a single underlying explanation.
        An important unresolved issue is the distinction between a representation’s
standalone strength and its complementary predictive value. A representation that is


                                             43
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


weak in isolation may still provide information useful for another predictor. Conversely,
a strong additional representation does not guarantee that a fusion mechanism can
effectively utilise that information.
       Reliability introduces a related source of uncertainty. Adaptive weighting is
justified when the available context can identify changes in unimodal prediction
reliability (Wei et al., 2026; Q. Zhang et al., 2023). The value of conditioning
information, its relationship to branch errors, and its comparison with
exposure-matched static alternatives must be investigated on a task-specific basis.
       External evaluation adds another boundary. Re-using a procedure with a new
dataset-specific fitting, re-using the learned representation weights and then evaluating
an unaltered predictor in a different domain are all different experimental questions.
You cannot just take the results of one and treat them as equivalent evidence of
generalisation.

Research gap

Existing work shows that multimodal representations and fusion techniques can improve
performance in industrial and robotic monitoring under certain experimental conditions.
The body of work also shows that additional observations and more complex fusion
techniques do not necessarily outperform a very good unimodal or static baseline. The
degree to which the results can be generalised across applications is limited by
differences in the prediction target, the quality of the representation, the nature of the
supervision, the training regime and the reliability of the inputs (Goodarzi et al., 2023;
Hua et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b; W. Wang et al., 2020; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).
       This thesis addresses the conditional effectiveness of multimodal fusion under
comparable, task-specific experimental conditions. It examines how representation
suitability, complementary predictive information, fusion complexity, and input
reliability interact. It also asks whether benefits observed in one study transfer to other
industrial and robotic monitoring tasks. Rather than assuming that one architecture is
universally best, the thesis identifies which approaches provide benefits under the
conditions tested and assesses how far those findings can be generalised.

It by no means suggests that multimodal fusion, adaptive weighting, or robust
industrial monitoring have not been examined previously.


2.8.3     Derived Research Design Requirements

The previous evidence and the remaining questions address five requirements for the
research design regarding multimodal monitoring architectures.
        These requirements take the findings from the literature and turn them into
evaluation criteria relevant to the issues of representation, fusion, robustness, and
methodology raised in the original research proposal.
        They provide an analytical framework for assessing the empirical work, rather
than claiming that every historical experiment was originally designed in accordance
with those exact specifications.


R1. Target validity and modality correspondence

The prediction target must have a clear physical or operational interpretation. Its origin,
observation horizon, and relationship to the available inputs must be explicitly defined.
        Native outcome annotations, independent reference measurements, and
analytically derived proxies must be distinguished. For multimodal observations, the
matching procedure must determine whether they correspond to a compatible event or
observation interval.
        This directly follows from the supervision and correspondence distinctions
discussed in Sections 2.2 and 2.3 (including the industrial studies that use independent
reference instruments or registered observations (L. Chen et al., 2023; Pandiyan et al.,
2022; Petrich et al., 2021)).


R2. Representation suitability under explicit fitting conditions

Modality-specific representations should be evaluated using clearly defined observation
windows, aggregation schemes, preprocessing, supervision, pretraining, and fitting


                                            46
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


budgets.
       Conventional references and learned representations should be compared on the
same prediction targets and assessment observations. Where possible, assess
complementary usefulness separately from standalone performance.
       This is a direct consequence of evidence for engineered features, transferred
visual representations, and patch-based temporal learning under a variety of
experimental conditions (Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023; Nie et al., 2023; Roth et al., 2022).
       It directly supports the representation investigation in SQ1.


R3. Incremental fusion value under comparable experimental conditions

Fusion architectures should be benchmarked against credible unimodal references and
simple integration approaches on the same assessment observations.
       The impact of any additional information should be disentangled, where possible,
from changes in model capacity, fitted probability transformations, calibration, and
training.
       For prediction-level stacking, we need grouped out-of-fold prediction
construction and appropriate separation of fitting and assessment. Incremental
within-fold ordering must be distinguished from pooled score alignment.
       A matched single-input second-stage control can further reduce ambiguity about
the need for any additional fitting. Where such a control is not available, the resulting
attribution boundary must remain explicit.
       This is a direct consequence of the positive and negative fusion comparisons and
the methodological underpinnings of stacked generalisation (Hua et al., 2026; Thoduka
et al., 2024b; Ting & Witten, 1999; W. Wang et al., 2020).
       It directly supports SQ2.


R4. Reliability-aware comparison of fusion mechanisms

Known modality absence, degraded (but available) inputs, temporal misalignment and
distribution shift should all be considered as separate reliability conditions.


                                             47
Chapter 2. Critical Literature Review


       The impacted observations, corruption procedures, severity, training exposure,
availability information and fallback policies all need to be documented.
       When evaluating adaptive weighting, it should be compared against appropriate
static alternatives and available unimodal references. A comparable level of exposure to
corruption is necessary to determine whether improvements result from adaptation or
from differences in training conditions.
       Clean performance should be used as a benchmark when assessing improvements
in robustness.
       This is a direct consequence of the missing input studies, the controlled industrial
degradation studies, and the theoretical underpinning of quality-aware weighting
(Altinses & Schwung, 2023; Deng et al., 2026; Huo et al., 2022; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).
       It directly supports SQ3.


R5. Methodological validity and bounded cross-dataset interpretation

Evaluation should account for dependence between observations, the prediction target,
class distribution, discrimination, calibration, uncertainty, computational requirements,
and reproducibility.
       Where group identifiers are available, assessment and model development
procedures should align with the relevant physical or operational grouping.
       External evaluations must be able to distinguish between dataset-specific
refitting, supervised representation transfer, and unchanged model generalisation.
Differences between datasets should be interpreted in terms of their targets, observation
windows, available information, and assessment protocols, rather than through a
common ranking of performance scores that are inherently incompatible.
       This is a direct consequence of the literature on assessment independence,
calibration, selection uncertainty, reproducibility and target domain access (Bengio &
Grandvalet, 2004; Guo et al., 2017; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023; Ovadia et al., 2019;
Pineau et al., 2021; Varma & Simon, 2006).
       It is primarily an SQ4 product and forms the basis on which the SQ1–SQ3

evaluations are carried out.

following technical and methodological chapters.
        Chapter 3 covers the representation and fusion elements pertinent to R2–R4.

3.1        Common Mathematical Notation and

           Prediction Formulation

Industrial monitoring tasks can be formulated as regression or classification problems
depending on the target of interest. Regression estimates a continuous quantity, such as
a derived condition indicator, whereas binary classification estimates whether an
observation belongs to a specified outcome class.
          An individual prediction unit may represent a gear experiment run, robotic
action, milling interval, or manipulation episode. Each prediction unit belongs to a
dependence group representing a relevant acquisition or operational grouping.
          The principal multimodal classification architectures consider visual and
machine-sensor observations. Investigations involving several sensor groups use an
equivalent formulation with an extended set of input branches.
          Each prediction unit is associated with its available observations, modality
availability indicators, optional process or measured-quality context, and prediction
target.
          For binary classification, the positive outcome is defined separately for each
empirical study. In REASSEMBLE, ViFailback, and HANDOVER, the positive
outcome represents failure. In MSM, it represents abnormal cutting under the adopted
target definition.
          A modality-specific encoder transforms the available observations into a
representation. This representation may consist of engineered statistical features or
learned embeddings. A prediction head subsequently transforms the representation into
a scalar regression estimate or classification score.
          For classification, a sigmoid function maps a real-valued score into the interval
between zero and one. Although this procedure produces a bounded probability
estimate, it does not automatically establish that the resulting predictions are
calibrated.
          Calibration requires a separate fitting or assessment procedure that examines the


                                              57
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


relationship between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes.
       The logit transformation, ℓ(p) = log[p/(1 − p)], converts a probability into its
corresponding log-odds. Numerical implementations require appropriate clipping at the
probability boundaries to avoid undefined logarithms.
       Fusion can occur at different points in this prediction process. Feature-level
fusion combines modality representations before the final prediction head, while
prediction-level fusion combines modality-specific scores or probabilities. The
distinction determines the information available to the fusion system and the
relationships it can learn.
       Each empirical study specifies its prediction target, input representations,
trainable components, calibration procedure, and fusion interface. This prevents
architectural terminology from obscuring differences between the implemented
predictive systems.



3.2     Visual Representations

Visual observations give information about object appearance, spatial configuration,
and changes occurring during a physical operation. Their predictive usefulness depends
on how well the selected representation captures the relevant observations.
       Industrial monitoring frequently involves limited independently acquired samples
relative to the capacity of modern visual architectures. Pretrained convolutional
representations therefore provide a reasonable candidate for extracting reusable spatial
information without fitting an entire visual encoder from the available target-specific
observations.
       The success of transferred intermediate CNN features in industrial inspection
supports investigating such representations, although their suitability for
completed-action classification requires separate evaluation (Roth et al., 2022).
       This section examines convolutional feature extraction, the component structure
of RT-DETR, temporal aggregation, and alternative encoder adaptation procedures.



                                            58
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


3.2.1     Convolutional Feature Extraction and Spatial Pooling

A convolutional encoder transforms an input image into hierarchical feature maps.
Earlier layers typically represent local visual patterns, while deeper layers can encode
more abstract spatial and semantic information.
        Each feature map consists of multiple channels with corresponding spatial
dimensions. Global average pooling converts each feature channel into a scalar by
averaging its spatial activations.
        The pooled representations obtained at different scales are concatenated to form
a frame-level descriptor. For an observation containing several selected frames, a
complete-window representation can subsequently be obtained through temporal
averaging.
        This procedure produces a condensed representation whose dimensionality
remains independent of the starting spatial feature-map resolution and the number of
sampled frames.
        Spatial pooling retains information about the average activation of each feature
channel but removes explicit spatial coordinates and local correspondence. Temporal
averaging adds another restriction because the resulting representation is independent
of frame order.
        The representation can therefore exploit differences in visual content across an
observation window without explicitly modelling the temporal sequence in which those
differences occur.
        A convolutional reference remains important when Transformer-based
components motivate the architectural investigation. The Vision Transformer study
shows that the relative effectiveness of convolutional and attention-based architectures
depends on the scale of available pretraining data, with convolutional inductive biases
remaining valuable in smaller-data settings (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021).
        Video Swin introduces local spatiotemporal attention and shifted windows as an
alternative for processing video sequences. Its computational resource requirements
depend on the evaluated temporal clips and spatial crops (Liu et al., 2022).


                                            59
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


        These architectures provide alternative mechanisms for retaining visual
information. Their published results motivate comparative investigation rather than
establishing greater performance than a frozen convolutional representation under the
sample availability and fitting budgets of this thesis.


3.2.2     RT-DETR Architecture and Component Roles

RT-DETR is a real-time object detection architecture that combines a convolutional
backbone, a hybrid encoder for multi-scale feature interaction, and a query-based
detection decoder (Y. Zhao et al., 2024).
        The convolutional backbone extracts hierarchical spatial features from the input
image. The hybrid encoder supports within-scale and cross-scale interaction between
visual features. The Transformer-based decoder uses object queries to produce
object-level representations, while the detection heads generate object classifications
and bounding-box predictions.
        A pretrained object detection checkpoint therefore contains components that can
be used for different downstream purposes. Its convolutional backbone can be extracted
and reused independently of the detection encoder, decoder, and prediction heads.
        This distinction is particularly important when understanding the role of
RT-DETR in a downstream classification architecture. Loading a Transformer-based
detection checkpoint does not establish that every component of the original detector
participates in the executed prediction path.
        The scientific rationale for investigating the backbone concerns the availability of
pretrained multiscale spatial features. The additional hybrid encoder provides a
separate candidate for investigating whether further spatial feature interaction improves
the downstream representation.
        The object detection performance reported by Y. Zhao et al. (2024) motivates
the investigation of these components but does not establish their predictive suitability
for industrial action outcomes.

3.2.3      Backbone Extraction Versus Hybrid-Encoder or

           Full-Detector Execution

The principal REASSEMBLE visual representation uses the ResNet18-vd convolutional
backbone contained in the pretrained RT-DETR-R18vd checkpoint, identified as
PekingU/rtdetr_r18vd.

separately trained binary classification head then predicts the recorded action outcome.
         The principal visual prediction path therefore processes input video frames

spatial pooling, temporal averaging, and binary classification.

bounding-box prediction heads are bypassed in this core experiment.

visual representation in which the backbone and hybrid encoder are both involved.

feature dimensions and fitting budgets.
         The PHM visual-regression investigation uses a different extraction procedure. It
invokes the Ultralytics prediction path with a frozen RT-DETR-L checkpoint and
collects three intermediate multiscale feature maps through registered hooks. Spatial
pooling produces a 768-dimensional visual representation.
         The recorded implementation additionally includes extraction latency as a
numerical covariate, resulting in a 769-dimensional input to the regression head.
Consequently, the PHM visual head receives both image-derived information and a
timing-related variable.

3.2.4     Frame Sampling, Feature Aggregation, and Temporal

          Modelling

Video observations contain spatial information within individual frames and temporal
information across successive frames. The representation must determine how these two
sources of information are retained.
        Fixed frame sampling provides a reproducible number of observations for each
prediction unit. It limits computational variation between clips of different lengths and
allows the subsequent prediction head to receive a fixed-dimensional representation.
        However, a fixed sampling procedure may overlook brief events, including short
contact changes or transient visual evidence of failure. The suitability of the sampling
strategy therefore depends on the duration and visibility of the phenomenon associated
with the prediction target.
        Temporal mean pooling aggregates frame descriptors without learning
relationships between their positions. This makes it a low-capacity reference for
predicting an outcome associated with a complete observation window.
        Alternative aggregation procedures can retain additional temporal structure.
Boundary pooling restricts the representation to frames occurring within a selected part
of an operation, while temporal bins preserve coarse information about the progression
of the observation window. Recurrent neural networks and temporal Transformers
provide more flexible mechanisms for learning order-dependent relationships.
        TimeSformer separates spatial and temporal attention and reports advantages

                                           62
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


for its divided attention mechanism over the other tested attention configurations on
Kinetics-400 and Something-Something V2 (Bertasius et al., 2021, Table 1). This
provides a scientific motivation for investigating temporal organization, although it does
not directly establish the suitability of a particular boundary-pooling rule.
        The principal REASSEMBLE representation uses sixteen deterministically
sampled hand-camera frames. Images are resized to a resolution of 640 × 640 pixels, and
their numerical values are divided by 255. No additional ImageNet mean and
standard-deviation normalization is applied in the recorded core procedure.
        These preprocessing choices describe the executed implementation rather than a
universal preprocessing requirement for RT-DETR.
        The comparison between whole-action averaging and boundary-aware pooling
investigates whether restricting the aggregation interval preserves more useful
information for completed-action classification. Because the prediction target is
associated with the completed action, the use of observations close to its conclusion
does not establish early-warning capability.
        The corresponding empirical comparisons are presented in Chapter 6.


3.2.5     Frozen Extraction and Visual Fine-Tuning

Pretrained visual representations can be adapted to a target task at different levels.
Frozen extraction retains the parameters learned during pretraining and fits only a
downstream prediction head. Partial fine-tuning modifies selected encoder components,
while full-backbone fine-tuning updates the complete selected visual encoder.
        Frozen extraction reduces the number of trainable parameters and allows
extracted representations to be cached. This can make repeated model fitting within
grouped evaluation procedures computationally feasible.
        Fine-tuning provides the opportunity to adapt visual features to the appearance
and operational characteristics of the target dataset. Its effectiveness nevertheless
depends on supervision, sample availability, and optimization conditions.
        Comparisons between frozen and adapted representations require consideration


                                            63
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


of normalization-layer states, augmentation, learning rates, early stopping, batch sizes,
and optimization steps. The number of training epochs alone does not establish that
two configurations received equivalent optimization opportunities.
       Generic COCO initialization, REASSEMBLE-supervised donor initialization,
and target-domain adaptation represent distinct interventions.
       A generic frozen encoder transfers representations learned during external
pretraining. A donor encoder introduces information learned through supervision on the
source task. Target-domain adaptation modifies the encoder using observations and
labels from the new task.
       These distinctions are relevant to the ViFailback experiments, which separately
investigate frozen generic representations, transferred REASSEMBLE representations,
and the completed portion of target-backbone adaptation.
       The existence of extracted or cached features establishes that the corresponding
extraction stage occurred. It does not independently establish that downstream
classification, fusion fitting, or assessment was completed.



3.3     Sensor and Time-Series Representations

Machine-sensor observations describe physical quantities and operational states through
sequential measurements. Their usefulness depends on how the available channels relate
to the prediction target and how their temporal information is represented.
       This thesis investigates two principal representation families: engineered
statistical summaries and learned patch-based temporal representations.
       The two families impose different restrictions on the information available to a
predictor. Statistical summaries retain selected properties of an observation window
while discarding much of its original temporal order. Learned sequence representations
offer greater flexibility in modelling ordered observations but introduce additional
fitting requirements.
       Their comparison therefore addresses whether explicit temporal modelling



                                            64
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


retains useful information beyond compact summaries and whether the two
representations provide complementary predictive value.


3.3.1     Statistical and Signal-Derived Features

Statistical feature extraction transforms a sequence of measurements into a compact set
of descriptors. Common features include measures of central tendency, dispersion,
amplitude, extrema, and temporal change.
        The mean describes the average measurement level, while the root-mean-square
amplitude summarizes the magnitude of the observations. Other summaries, including
standard deviation, quantiles, range, and linear trend, describe different properties of
the observed measurements.
        Such features provide a deliberate restriction on the information available to the
prediction model. They can represent operating states without requiring a high-capacity
sequence encoder to be fitted using limited independently acquired observations.
        Conventional engineered features remain scientifically relevant in industrial
applications. For example, classical local geometric representations have demonstrated
competitive performance in industrial 3D anomaly detection, supporting the need to
retain strong engineered references when evaluating learned representations (Horwitz &
Hoshen, 2023).
        In REASSEMBLE, ten statistical features are calculated for each of the 22
progress-resampled sensor channels. These features include the mean, standard
deviation, median, minimum, maximum, lower quartile, upper quartile, range,
root-mean-square amplitude, and linear progress slope.
        The mean and median describe the central measurement level, while the
standard deviation and quartiles characterize its distribution. The minimum, maximum,
and range describe the observed extrema and their separation. The root-mean-square
amplitude represents measurement magnitude, and the linear progress slope describes
coarse directional change across the action.
        The resulting feature vector contains 220 features. These features retain


                                            65
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


information concerning location, spread, magnitude, and coarse trend while discarding
most detailed temporal ordering.
        The available sensor channels include force, torque, gripper-related
measurements, joint effort, and joint velocity. Their statistical representation tests
whether compact descriptions of the completed action provide suﬀicient information for
the prediction target.
        The comparison does not assume that temporal information is irrelevant. The
subsequent PatchTST and statistical-feature combination investigates whether the
learned temporal representation contains complementary predictive information.
        Spectral representations provide another approach to summarizing sequential
measurements. Their interpretation depends on the physical sampling rate and
preprocessing interval. A sequence resampled according to action progress is not
equivalent to a uniformly sampled physical vibration signal. Frequency-domain
interpretations must therefore respect the acquisition and resampling procedures.
        MSM uses a separate representation derived from its controller measurements,
while PHM uses minute-level sensor and process-context features. These representations
are defined according to their respective measurement systems and prediction targets
rather than treated as interchangeable feature vectors.


3.3.2     PatchTST Architecture

PatchTST introduces a patch-based approach to time-series representation learning.
Instead of treating every individual timestamp as a separate Transformer token, the
architecture divides a sequence into local subsequences called patches (Nie et al., 2023).
        Each patch is transformed into a learned embedding. The resulting embedding
sequence is processed by Transformer layers that can model relationships between
different temporal positions.
        The original PatchTST architecture incorporates channel-independent processing
with shared model parameters across channels. This structure allows temporal
relationships within individual channels to be represented without requiring a separate


                                            66
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


independently parameterized Transformer for every measurement channel.
        Patching provides two potential advantages. First, local subsequences retain
information about neighbouring measurements within each patch. Second, representing
a sequence through patches reduces the number of tokens processed by the attention
mechanism relative to a representation in which every timestamp is treated separately.
        The original work indicates the relevance of this approach for time-series
forecasting (Nie et al., 2023). Its use in this thesis concerns a different prediction
problem: supervised condition estimation or outcome classification.
        The investigated PatchTST implementations therefore adapt the underlying
architectural principles to their respective targets. They are initialized from scratch
rather than transferring pretrained PatchTST weights.
        This distinction is necessary because evaluating a scratch-trained
Transformer-based encoder does not establish the effectiveness of pretrained time-series
representation transfer.


3.3.3      Patching, Sequence Length, Padding, and Aggregation

The number of patches generated from a time series depends on the sequence length,
patch width, stride, and padding procedure.
        The patch width determines how many successive observations are included
within each token, while the stride determines the displacement between consecutive
patches.
        For overlapping patches, the stride is smaller than the patch width. This allows
neighbouring tokens to share observations and represent partially overlapping temporal
structures.
        Padding and masking procedures can change the number of generated patches
and the observations available to the representation. Consequently, the theoretical
patch count must be distinguished from the number of tokens actually processed by a
particular implementation.
        A patch embedding projects the values within a subsequence into a hidden


                                             67
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


representation. Positional information and Transformer blocks subsequently support the
processing of relationships between patches.
          After temporal encoding, a pooling or aggregation procedure transforms the
patch-level representations into a fixed-dimensional descriptor suitable for downstream
prediction.
          The core REASSEMBLE implementation resamples each action to 512 progress
positions across 22 sensor channels. The scratch-trained PatchTST classifier uses a
patch width of 32, a stride of 16, a hidden representation width of 32, two Transformer
layers, four attention heads, and a feed-forward width of 64.

3.3.4     Representation Learning Versus Direct Prediction

A learned encoder can be used for different purposes within a prediction architecture. It
may provide a reusable representation for a downstream model, directly predict a
target, or supply features to a multimodal fusion mechanism.
        These roles determine how supervision enters the modelling procedure.
        A generic pretrained encoder can generate fixed features without using the labels
associated with the target assessment population. Such representations can be cached if

                                            70
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


their preprocessing and sample identities remain consistent.
        In contrast, an encoder trained using target-specific supervision becomes part of
the fitted prediction system. Its training must therefore respect the evaluation
partitions associated with the downstream task.
        Fitting a supervised encoder using the complete labelled population before
constructing evaluation folds can introduce target information into the resulting
representations, even if the final prediction head is subsequently fitted within each fold.
        The evaluation must consequently account for which samples influence
preprocessing, representation learning, prediction-head fitting, calibration, and model
selection.
        The distinction also applies to the statistical-feature and PatchTST comparison.
Both representations originate from the same physical sensor observations. Any
improvement obtained by combining them concerns representation complementarity
rather than the contribution of an additional independently acquired modality.



3.4      Prediction Models and Reference Baselines

Reference predictors provide the basis for determining whether an investigated
representation or fusion mechanism contributes measurable predictive value.
        A suitable comparison requires more than a weak baseline. The reference should
represent a credible alternative under the available data and fitting conditions, enabling
the contribution of additional information or model complexity to be investigated.
        The thesis therefore considers constant predictors, regularized linear models,
neural prediction heads, and nonlinear conventional classifiers.


3.4.1        Constant and Simple Reference Predictors

Constant predictors establish a minimum reference for determining whether a learned
model improves on a simple description of the training target distribution.
        For regression, the training mean minimizes squared error within the training


                                            71
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


population. The training median minimizes absolute error.
        The principal PHM comparison retains the training mean as its recorded
historical reference. The mathematical suitability of the median for absolute error does
not imply that a median-based comparison was executed.
        For binary classification, a constant probability can be estimated from the
positive-outcome prevalence in the training population. Such a reference helps
distinguish discrimination from performance associated with the target distribution.
        Context-only predictors provide another useful reference. Process identity, action
duration, or time index may contain predictive information even when the principal
sensor observations are unavailable.
        For example, action identity may be associated with different failure frequencies,
while a condition proxy may vary with experiment progression. A context-only reference
can therefore help assess whether an apparent improvement requires additional physical
measurements or can be partly explained by contextual predictability.


3.4.2     Linear and Logistic Models

Regularized linear models provide compact prediction mechanisms that can be fitted
without learning high-capacity representations.
        Ridge regression estimates a continuous target by minimizing squared prediction
error together with a squared coeﬀicient penalty.
        For binary classification, logistic regression represents the probability of the
positive outcome by applying a sigmoid transformation to a linear combination of input
features and a fitted intercept.
        The model is generally fitted using a cross-entropy objective, with regularization
controlling the magnitude of its coeﬀicients.
        Its performance depends on preprocessing, feature standardization,
regularization, class weighting, and solver configuration.
        In the principal REASSEMBLE statistical-sensor comparison, the regularization
parameter is selected from three candidate values: 0.1, 1, and 10. The selection is


                                             72
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


performed within the permitted training partitions.
        Logistic regression can also serve as a prediction-level fusion mechanism when its
inputs are the probabilities produced by separately fitted branch models. Such a model
is a logistic stacker rather than a sensor representation.
        The core F2 stacker uses a fixed regularization parameter of 1. This value
belongs to the recorded implementation configuration and is not described as the
outcome of a separate hyperparameter search.


3.4.3      Neural Classification and Regression Heads

A neural prediction head maps an extracted representation to the desired regression or
classification output.
        A compact feed-forward head may first transform the input representation
through an aﬀine layer followed by a nonlinear activation function. Additional
regularization, including dropout during training, can be applied before the final scalar
prediction layer.
        The prediction head allows nonlinear relationships between representation
features and the target to be learned without necessarily modifying the underlying
encoder.
        In the core REASSEMBLE experiments, the visual and PatchTST classification
heads are fitted using class-weighted binary cross-entropy. The original
context-conditioned gates instead use unweighted binary cross-entropy.
        The weighting procedure is therefore another difference between the evaluated
fitting configurations.
        The core visual prediction head contains 114,945 trainable parameters in
addition to the frozen convolutional backbone.
        The corresponding PatchTST sensor module contains 109,505 trainable
parameters across its encoder, projection, and classification components. Of these,
90,369 belong to the projection and classifier.
        These counts describe the fitted capacity of the respective configurations. They


                                            73
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


do not independently establish equivalent computational cost or optimization diﬀiculty.

Feature-level fusion combines modality representations before the final prediction stage.

3.5.2     Uniform Prediction Averaging

Uniform averaging provides a simple prediction-level fusion mechanism.
        The core F1 baseline assigns equal importance to the visual and sensor
probability estimates by calculating their arithmetic mean.
        The integration rule contains no fitted combination parameters, although its
component predictors and their calibration procedures are fitted using the respective
training observations.
        This makes F1 a useful comparator for investigating whether learning a more
flexible integration mechanism produces additional predictive value.
        The averaging of probabilities is distinct from averaging logits and subsequently
applying a sigmoid. These procedures generally produce different probability estimates.
        Furthermore, averaging two calibrated probability estimates does not
automatically establish that the combined probability remains calibrated.
        When one input is unavailable, the averaging rule requires an additional policy
specifying whether the surviving prediction is returned, the weights are renormalized, or
the system abstains.

3.5.3     Logistic Stacking

Stacked generalization provides a methodological foundation for learning a second-stage
prediction model from the outputs of first-stage predictors. Ting and Witten (1999)
investigate the construction of this learning problem using cross-validated predictions,
allowing the second-stage model to learn from outputs obtained for observations
excluded from the corresponding first-stage fitting.
        The original publication evaluates a different combination model from the
logistic stacker investigated in this thesis. Its methodological contribution motivates the
construction of a second-stage prediction problem rather than establishing the
suitability of the exact F2 configuration.
        The core F2 mechanism combines the calibrated visual and sensor probabilities
using a logistic prediction model. Its fitted parameters consist of an intercept and two
branch coeﬀicients. The weighted combination of the branch probabilities and the
intercept is transformed through a sigmoid function.


                             pF2 = sigmoid(b + αv pv + αs ps ).                        (3.1)

        Here, pv and ps are the visual and sensor probabilities, b is the intercept, and αv
and αs are their fitted coeﬀicients.
        Unlike a convex weighted average, the branch coeﬀicients are not required to be
positive or sum to one. The stacker can therefore learn unequal contributions from its
inputs while adjusting the resulting prediction through its intercept and logistic
transformation.
        Its three-parameter structure provides a compact mechanism for investigating
whether a fitted combination improves on equal probability averaging.
        The coeﬀicients must not be interpreted as normalized modality importance
scores or causal estimates of the information contributed by each physical observation.

models that exclude the corresponding observation or dependence group from their

predictive information and those of an additional fitted probability transformation.
           A strictly increasing transformation of a sensor score preserves within-fold
ranking. Fold-specific transformations can nevertheless change ranking after predictions
from different folds are pooled. Chapter 4 establishes the methodological implications of
this distinction, while Chapter 6 examines its relevance to the evaluated F2 model.

3.5.5     Quality-Conditioned Interactions and Adaptive

          Extensions

Quality-aware fusion is motivated by situations in which the reliability or predictive
usefulness of a modality changes across observations.
        The theoretical investigation of dynamic fusion by Q. Zhang et al. (2023)

under which adaptive weighting may provide benefits.
        These conditions motivate the use of context-dependent information but do not
establish that an arbitrary measured-quality descriptor provides an accurate estimate of
predictive reliability.

context-dependent adjustment can improve a previously fitted static fusion model.

methods do not consistently dominate across all evaluated conditions (Ovadia et al.,
2019).

robustness under degradation represent distinct empirical findings.


3.5.6      Missing-Modality Routing and Fallback

Modality absence represents a different reliability problem from degradation of an input
that remains available.
         When one branch is unavailable and the other remains accessible, the masked


                                             81
Chapter 3. Technical Foundations and Possible Models


normalization of the original context-conditioned gate assigns a weight of one to the
surviving branch.
       The original REASSEMBLE robustness evaluator explicitly returns the
surviving branch probability and applies that branch’s threshold. If both branches are
unavailable, the system rejects the input.
       This procedure establishes deterministic fallback behaviour.
       If different fusion mechanisms return the same surviving branch prediction, their
performance under known absence is identical for those fixed branch outputs. Such a
comparison evaluates the fallback policy rather than demonstrating a benefit from
learned adaptive weighting.
       Training-time modality dropout may influence the behaviour of a model when
the available inputs change. Nevertheless, training exposure to missing observations
must be distinguished from an empirically measured improvement under a specified
absence condition.
       ModDrop introduces training mechanisms that expose multimodal models to
unavailable inputs (Neverova et al., 2015). EmbraceNet investigates stochastic feature
selection together with modality-availability information (Choi & Lee, 2019).
Missing-modality Transformer studies further examine architectures trained and
evaluated under incomplete-input conditions (M. Ma et al., 2022).
       Distillation provides an alternative in which information from multiple modalities
during training is used to improve a model deployed with fewer inputs. Imputation
instead estimates a replacement for an unavailable observation or representation.
       These mechanisms address different acquisition and deployment conditions.
Their suitability requires comparison with a predictor trained directly on the available
observations.
       The explicit fallback investigated in this thesis provides a defined response to
known absence. Recognizing unreliable but available observations remains a separate
adaptive-fusion question.

This thesis investigates multimodal methods for industrial monitoring, asking whether

4.4     Dataset Admission and Preparation Checks

Before an experiment, the available data were checked against the prediction task and
assessment protocol.
       The sample manifest records each prediction unit, target source, physical or
operational group, input path, observation boundaries, available modalities, and any
reason for exclusion.
       The manifest also establishes the relationship between the source dataset
population and the subset admitted to a particular experimental comparison.
       Visual preparation checks examine whether observations can be decoded,
whether suﬀicient frames are available, whether the image geometry is compatible with
the extraction procedure, and whether the relevant process is represented within the
viewing window.
       An image may be technically readable yet provide no visible evidence of the
target. Image quality and target visibility must therefore be assessed separately.
       Sensor preparation checks examine channel ordering, physical units, timestamp
consistency, missing observations, repeated measurements, and how interpolation or
aggregation procedures are interpreted.
       Resampling is particularly important when measurements are collected at
diverse temporal resolutions or when sequences are transformed into a common
action-progress representation.
       These transformations must be recorded because they affect which properties of
the source physical measurements remain available to the model.



                                           96
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


       Pairing checks whether observations from different sources belong to the same
prediction unit. The mere presence of a video and a sensor file does not establish that
they represent the same event or time interval.
       Each admitted pair therefore requires a defined correspondence procedure.
       Exclusions can influence the resulting class distribution and the diﬀiculty of the
assessment population. Removing corrupted observations or insuﬀiciently represented
failures may change the scientific interpretation of the comparison.

4.5      Target Provenance, Prediction Units, and

         Available Inputs

A scientifically interpretable prediction task requires an explicit definition of what is
predicted, how its reference value is obtained, and which observations are available at
the intended prediction time.
        A model’s predictive performance alone cannot establish that its target
accurately represents the underlying physical or operational phenomenon.
        This distinction is particularly relevant to the PHM investigation. Its
image-derived candidate-area measure represents a provisional gear-condition proxy.
        Predicting the proxy from machine-sensor observations measures agreement with
the constructed target. It does not independently establish the accuracy of physical
wear estimation, tooth-damage measurement, or remaining-useful-life prediction.
        The robotic and machining studies use different operational outcomes.
        In PHM, the principal prediction unit is a gear experiment run, and the target is
a photograph-derived continuous condition proxy. REASSEMBLE predicts the recorded
success or failure of completed robotic assembly actions. MSM predicts abnormal
cutting within milling observation intervals. ViFailback predicts the recorded success or
failure of complete manipulation episodes, while HANDOVER investigates binary
outcomes associated with human–robot interaction trials.
        The MSM investigation retains its declared mapping of native milling labels.
Alternative mappings are considered through sensitivity analyses because changing the
interpretation of a native label also changes the prediction task.
        The meaning of the reported performance measures therefore depends on the
target definition associated with each study.


4.5.1     Observation Windows and Prediction Horizons

The observation window determines the information available to the prediction model.
        Sampling visual frames across a complete robotic action can provide information


                                             98
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


about its concluding state. Similarly, sensor statistics calculated over an entire milling
interval describe observations collected throughout that interval.
        These representations support retrospective assessment of condition or outcome.
On their own, they do not show that a model can make a useful prediction before the
operation is complete.
        Prospective monitoring would require a defined decision time, causal
preprocessing, a specified lead-time measure, and target annotations appropriate to the
intended prediction horizon.
        The retrospective experiments conducted in this thesis do not measure
prospective warning time.
        The distinction also influences computational interpretation. A model may
produce a prediction rapidly after receiving its input, while acquiring the required
observation window introduces additional delay.
        The total time required for an operational prediction therefore includes both
observation acquisition and the subsequent processing and inference stages.


4.5.2     Contextual Information and Predictive Shortcuts

Process identity, observation duration, time index, and other contextual variables can
contain predictive information independently of the principal physical observations.
        Such variables may provide useful context, but they can also introduce
alternative explanations for apparent improvements.
        For example, the extraction latency included in the PHM visual regression
representation introduces a timing-related covariate alongside image-derived
information.
        Similarly, an action-conditioned fusion mechanism may exploit differences in
failure prevalence between action categories. Its predictive improvement would not
automatically establish that it recognised a deterioration in physical sensor quality.
        Context-only references and constant-adjustment controls provide ways to
investigate these alternative explanations where the corresponding comparisons are

Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


available.
       The role of each contextual variable must therefore be interpreted in light of its
relationship to the prediction target, acquisition procedure, and intended architectural
function.



4.6     Grouped Assessment, Data Splits, and Leakage

        Prevention

Reliable evaluation requires separating the observations used to develop a model from
those used to assess its predictive performance.
       Industrial and robotic observations often exhibit dependence because several
samples originate from the same physical experiment, recording, task, machine,
participant, or acquisition session.
       Randomly partitioning individual observations may therefore place closely
related samples into both development and assessment populations.
       Such overlap can produce an evaluation that does not represent the intended
level of generalization.
       Grouped assessment prevents overlap at the specified dependence-group level. It
does not, by itself, establish independence at higher physical or operational levels.
       The appropriate grouping therefore depends on the scientific claim associated
with the prediction task.
       The principal PHM sensor protocol holds out complete gear experiments, while
REASSEMBLE groups actions by recording. MSM examines experiment-based and
acquisition-date groupings, and ViFailback separates manipulation tasks. HANDOVER
retains the published oﬀicial trial partitions.
       The HANDOVER dataset publication describes its oﬀicial participant-disjoint
assessment. However, the available participant-to-trial correspondence does not permit
independent reconstruction of that grouping or the construction of participant-disjoint
inner training folds.


                                            100
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


        For MSM, an acquisition date may include multiple tools or operators. Physical
equipment may also recur across dates. Consequently, date-disjoint assessment does not
establish generalisation to an entirely new machine, factory, or installation.
        A corresponding generalisation claim requires grouping information at the
relevant physical level.


4.6.1     Training-Side Preprocessing and Model Selection

Within each outer assessment split, all data-dependent modelling operations must be
considered part of model development.
        These operations include feature scaling and normalisation, imputation
parameters estimated from data, feature selection, supervised representation learning,
hyperparameter selection, probability calibration, decision-threshold selection, and
learned fusion fitting.
        Separating these procedures from the assessment population is necessary because
information can enter a prediction system through preprocessing or model selection
even when the final classifier excludes the assessment labels.
        Using cross-validation to select model configurations can produce optimistic
error estimates if the same evaluation evidence is later used to report the selected
performance (Varma & Simon, 2006).
        More generally, leakage must be assessed through the complete modelling
procedure, not only through the final training operation (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023).
        When a representation is derived using a fixed, generic pretrained encoder
without target-specific supervision, its features may be cached before target-specific
model fitting. However, any data-dependent normalisation, supervised adaptation, or
feature selection must respect the corresponding permitted development population.


4.6.2     Out-of-Fold Predictions and Learned Fusion

Prediction-level stacking requires a second-stage training population containing the
outputs of its first-stage predictors.

                                           101
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


       Using predictions generated from observations already included in first-stage
fitting can provide the stacker with an unrealistically favourable description of branch
performance.
       Stacked generalisation therefore motivates constructing second-stage training
inputs through cross-validated first-stage predictions (Ting & Witten, 1999).
       In the grouped REASSEMBLE setting, the corresponding dependence group is
the recording.
       For each outer assessment split, the outer training population is partitioned into
grouped inner fitting and prediction subsets. Branch models generate predictions for
observations whose corresponding recordings were excluded from their fitting.
       These predictions form an out-of-fold prediction bank used by subsequent
training-side calibration, model-selection, and fusion-fitting procedures according to the
relevant protocol.
       The final selected branch models and prediction-level combination are then used
to generate predictions for the designated outer assessment recordings.
       The original REASSEMBLE stacker contains an additional methodological
qualification.
       Within each outer split, the stacker’s internal validation reuses an out-of-fold
prediction bank generated from the outer training recordings.
       Although each predicted row originates from a branch that excluded its own
recording, a branch used to generate a prediction for a stacker training row may have
been fitted on recordings subsequently assigned to a stacker validation subset.
       This introduces a dependency within the outer training population.
       The outer assessment recordings remain excluded from fitting. The shared
prediction bank therefore limits claims of strict independence at the deeper
meta-validation stage without, by itself, establishing leakage from the outer assessment
population.
       This distinction matters: an evaluation may keep outer assessment data separate
while using a less strictly nested procedure for internal model selection.

4.7     Model Fitting, Selection, Calibration, and

        Threshold Procedures

Evaluating representation and fusion mechanisms depends on the procedures through
which their parameters are estimated, and their predictions are converted into decisions.
       Each empirical comparison therefore identifies its trainable components,
initialisation procedure, preprocessing, candidate configurations, training budget, and
relevant stopping or selection conditions.
       The experimental programme includes original baseline comparisons, exploratory
pilots, controlled follow-ups, and matched short-budget investigations.
       Several more extensive experiments completed only a subset of their originally
planned configurations. Their interpretation is restricted to the comparisons for which
the required fitting and assessment stages were completed.
       The prediction procedure distinguishes four operations: fitting model parameters,
selecting a model, calibrating probabilities, and selecting a decision threshold.




                                             105
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


4.7.1     Model Fitting and Selection

Model fitting estimates the parameters of the selected prediction architecture using its
permitted training observations.
        Model selection subsequently determines which representation, hyperparameter
configuration, or fitting procedure is retained for assessment.
        These operations can involve different data subsets and different criteria.
        Equating the number of training epochs does not necessarily mean equivalent
optimisation exposure if the configurations differ in batch size, available observations,
early stopping, initialisation, or trainable components.
        Similarly, a pretrained frozen representation and a scratch-trained temporal
encoder receive different forms of prior information and allocate computational
resources differently.
        The evaluation therefore retains the actual fitting procedures associated with
each comparison rather than treating equal epoch counts as a complete definition of
experimental fairness.


4.7.2     Probability Calibration

Probability calibration concerns the relationship between predicted probabilities and
observed outcomes.
        A model can discriminate effectively between positive and negative observations
while producing poorly calibrated probability estimates.
        Calibration methods use development-side predictions and corresponding
outcomes to adjust the numerical interpretation of prediction scores. Validation-based
calibration can improve probability quality under appropriate conditions, but its
effectiveness must be assessed rather than assumed (Guo et al., 2017).
        The principal REASSEMBLE branch calibrators use sigmoid logistic maps fitted
to selected inner out-of-fold prediction scores.
        The subsequent fusion mechanisms apply different probability transformations.
        F2 introduces a fitted logistic map with a free intercept and separate branch

                                            106
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


coeﬀicients. It does not include an additional post-stacker calibration stage in the
recorded core implementation.
         The original context-conditioned gates combine branch logits through normalised
weights and apply a sigmoid to the resulting weighted value. They do not include a free
final intercept or an additional post-gate calibrator.
         These differences influence the numerical outputs of the evaluated prediction
systems and can affect their discrimination and probability quality.


4.7.3      Within-Fold Ranking and Pooled Prediction Scores

The effect of a score transformation depends on the level at which predictions are
evaluated.
         A strictly increasing transformation preserves the ordering of observations within
a fixed assessment population. Consequently, it preserves ranking-based metrics such as
average precision and AUROC within that population, subject to numerical precision
and tie handling.
         When a sensor probability is transformed through a logistic function with a
positive sensor coeﬀicient, its ordering remains unchanged within the corresponding fold.
         However, independently fitted transformations can differ between assessment
folds.
         When predictions from different folds are pooled, these transformations may
change the relative ordering of observations originating from different folds, even though
the ordering within each fold remains unchanged.
         Consequently, pooled average precision may change even when the ranking
within every individual fold remains unchanged.
         This distinction is particularly relevant when evaluating learned fusion against a
constituent unimodal predictor.
         A comparison can involve both additional information from another prediction
branch and an additional fitted transformation of the existing score.
         Within-fold discrimination, pooled ranking, and probability calibration therefore

Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


represent distinct effects.
        A matched sensor-only second-stage predictor provides an additional control for
investigating the contribution of the fitted transformation. Where such a separately
fitted control is unavailable, arithmetic analysis of the retained model can provide
narrower evidence about the contribution of particular terms without resolving the
complete attribution problem.
        The corresponding REASSEMBLE saved-prediction investigation is presented in
Chapter 6.


4.7.4     Decision-Threshold Selection

Binary classification requires a decision rule that converts prediction probabilities or
scores into outcome labels.
        Observations with prediction scores at or above the selected threshold are
classified as positive, while those below the threshold are classified as negative.
        The threshold influences the number of observations classified as positive and
therefore changes sensitivity, specificity, precision, and false-positive behaviour.
        The original implementation examines a threshold grid ranging from 0.01 to 0.99
using permitted training-side predictions and a specified selection objective, such as
balanced accuracy.
        A threshold selected in this way belongs to the experimental evaluation protocol.
It is not necessarily an operationally optimal industrial alarm threshold.
        Industrial decision-making may assign different costs to missed failures, false
alarms, interruptions, or unnecessary maintenance. Establishing an operationally
appropriate threshold would require information about these consequences and the
intended monitoring application.
        The empirical examinations therefore distinguish predictive discrimination from
decisions obtained using the specified threshold-selection procedure.

4.8      Metrics and Uncertainty Estimation

The evaluation metrics used throughout the thesis reflect the different prediction targets
and scientific questions addressed by the empirical studies.
        Regression error, classification discrimination, probability quality, and
threshold-dependent decisions describe different properties of a prediction model.
        Their interpretation also depends on the target definition, class distribution,
assessment population, and aggregation procedure.


4.8.1     Regression Metrics

Regression performance is evaluated using errors between the predicted and observed
continuous target values.
        Mean absolute error describes the average absolute difference between predicted
and observed target values. Root mean squared error calculates the square root of the
average squared prediction error.
        Both measures retain the units of the prediction target.
        Mean absolute error describes the average magnitude of prediction deviations,
while root mean squared error assigns greater influence to larger prediction errors
through its squared-error formulation.
        The coeﬀicient of determination compares prediction error against the variation
of the observed target values. It may become negative when the fitted predictor
performs poorly relative to its reference denominator and can be unstable when the
variation within an assessment group is limited.
        In PHM, the physical interpretation of these metrics depends on the validity of
the photograph-derived condition measure.
        Low error establishes predictive agreement with the constructed target rather
than independently verified physical damage estimation.
        Tooth-level and run-level regression measurements are reported separately
because they represent different prediction units and dependence structures.



                                            109
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


4.8.2     Classification Metrics

Binary classification can be evaluated using threshold-dependent decisions and
ranking-based performance measures.
        Accuracy describes the proportion of correctly classified observations. Precision
measures the proportion of positive predictions corresponding to actual positive
outcomes. Recall measures the proportion of actual positives identified by the classifier.
        Specificity measures the proportion of negative observations correctly classified,
while balanced accuracy gives equal importance to sensitivity and specificity.
        F1 combines precision and recall through their harmonic mean.
        These threshold-dependent measures describe the decisions produced by a
specified classification threshold.
        Ranking-based measures instead examine how well the model orders positive and
negative observations across possible thresholds.
        The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUROC, evaluates
discrimination between positive and negative observations.
        Average precision summarises the precision–recall relationship and provides a
useful measure for classification tasks involving imbalanced outcomes. Precision–recall
analysis can reveal aspects of predictive performance that are less apparent from
ROC-based interpretation when one outcome class is relatively uncommon (Saito &
Rehmsmeier, 2015).
        Macro average precision additionally gives equal importance to the class-specific
average precision values. Probability quality is examined through measures such as the
Brier score and expected calibration error, which respectively characterise squared
probability error and discrepancies between confidence and observed outcome
frequencies.
        The metrics available for each study depend on its target, prediction outputs,
and completed evaluation protocol.
        Their inclusion in the shared methodological framework does not imply that
every metric was calculated for every experiment.


                                            110
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


4.8.3     Average Precision and Outcome Prevalence

The principal classification ranking metric is average precision, calculated using the
average_precision_score implementation.
        Average precision summarises the precision–recall relationship by weighting
precision values by their corresponding increments in recall.
        This formulation is distinct from estimating the area under a precision–recall
curve through trapezoidal numerical integration.
        Average precision depends on the prevalence of the designated positive class.
        If a binary predictor assigns the same score to every observation, its average
precision equals the positive-class prevalence in the assessment population.
        Thus, the same absolute average precision value can have different implications
under different outcome distributions.
        A high failure average precision may provide limited evidence of discrimination
when failure already represents the majority of the evaluated observations.
        This principle is particularly relevant to the ViFailback assessment, where failure
constitutes more than 85% of the principal eligible population.
        Reporting only failure average precision can therefore obscure the model’s ability
to identify successful outcomes.
        Where both outcomes are relevant, class-specific average precision can be
calculated separately for success and failure. Their binary macro average is obtained by
calculating the arithmetic mean of these two class-specific values.
        The macro average assigns equal importance to the two class-specific ranking
measures rather than allowing the majority outcome alone to determine the reported
summary.
        For a constant-score binary predictor, the both-class macro average precision
reference equals 0.5 when both outcome classes are present.
        The interpretation of class-specific and macro-average precision must
nevertheless retain the associated target definition and assessment population.
        REASSEMBLE principally reports failure average precision. MSM reports


                                            111
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


abnormal-cutting average precision. ViFailback additionally reports both-class macro
average precision.
        These are distinct evaluation quantities and must not be interpreted as directly
comparable numerical measures of architectural performance across the datasets.


4.8.4     Metric Aggregation Across Folds and Seeds

Grouped cross-validation produces predictions from separately fitted models associated
with different assessment partitions.
        Performance can be summarised through metrics calculated within individual
folds, averages of those fold-level metrics, or metrics calculated after combining the
held-out predictions.
        Mean-fold average precision is calculated by obtaining the average precision for
each assessment fold and subsequently averaging those values.
        Pooled average precision instead combines the corresponding held-out prediction
vectors and calculates average precision over the combined population.
        Because average precision is nonlinear, these aggregation procedures generally
produce different numerical results.
        The pooled value can also depend on how scores align across separately fitted
fold models.
        The original REASSEMBLE primary comparison reports pooled failure average
precision across its held-out recording partitions.
        MSM reports abnormal-cutting average precision averaged over seed-specific
pooled predictions.
        ViFailback distinguishes failure average precision, success average precision, and
their both-class macro average.
        Reported seed variation and fold variation must also remain separate. Different
random initialisations or fitting seeds represent another source of experimental variation
rather than additional independently acquired assessment observations.
        All reported comparisons therefore retain the metric definition, positive-class


                                            112
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


convention, aggregation procedure, seed configuration, and assessment population
associated with the original result.


4.8.5     Probability Quality and Calibration

Probability-based assessment examines whether numerical prediction probabilities
provide a useful description of observed outcome frequencies.
        The Brier score measures the average squared difference between predicted
probabilities and observed binary outcomes.
        Unlike ranking-based measures, the Brier score is sensitive to the numerical
probability values assigned to observations.
        Proper scoring rules provide a principled basis for assessing probability forecasts
rather than only their ranking or thresholded decisions (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007).
        Expected calibration error provides a complementary description based on
discrepancies between predicted confidence and observed outcome frequencies across
probability bins.
        Its interpretation depends on the number and construction of bins, the definition
of confidence, and the available observations within each bin.
        A classifier with strong ranking performance may still assign poorly calibrated
probabilities. Conversely, a model with improved probability error may not provide
better discrimination.
        The evaluation therefore distinguishes these properties rather than treating
calibration, average precision, AUROC, and threshold-dependent classification as
interchangeable indicators of reliability.
        Probability calibration can also change under altered input conditions. Evidence
from clean-data calibration must therefore be interpreted separately from probability
quality under degradation or distribution shift (Ovadia et al., 2019).

4.8.6     Paired Model Comparisons and Uncertainty Estimation

The scientific interpretation of a performance difference requires consideration of its
magnitude and associated uncertainty.
        For two prediction models evaluated on the same observations, a paired
comparison can be obtained by subtracting the average precision of the reference model
from that of the investigated model.
        A positive difference indicates higher average precision for the investigated model
on that assessment population, while a negative difference indicates higher average
precision for the reference model.
        The paired construction retains the common observations and corresponding
outcomes for the two models. This supports a more direct comparison than interpreting
their performance estimates independently.
        The uncertainty analyses use cluster-based bootstrap procedures where
supported by the retained experimental records.
        A cluster bootstrap resamples the specified dependence groups with replacement,
rather than resampling individual observations independently. It then recalculates the
paired metric difference for each resampled population.
        The core REASSEMBLE reports use 2,000 recording-level bootstrap replicates.
        A PHM report uses 10,000 run-cluster bootstrap replicates over 20 runs from
three gear experiments.
        The latter resampling procedure requires particular care because runs from the
same gear experiment may retain higher-level dependence. Run-level resampling does
not automatically capture all dependence between observations originating from the
same physical experiment.
        The reported interval remains associated with the original run-cluster procedure,
and its interpretation must retain this restriction.
        Increasing the number of bootstrap replicates reduces the numerical variability
of the estimated resampling distribution. It does not increase the number of
independently acquired physical experiments.


                                            114
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


       Similarly, the MSM date-cluster intervals rely on a particularly limited
population of five acquisition dates. Their interpretation remains conditional on those
groups and the recorded fitted models.
       Cross-validation folds also share training observations. They should therefore not
be treated as independent experimental replications when calculating uncertainty.
Bengio and Grandvalet (2004) establish the limitations of universally unbiased variance
estimation for k-fold cross-validation, supporting caution when constructing uncertainty
estimates from overlapping training populations.
       The retained bootstrap intervals characterise uncertainty under the selected
resampling unit and fitted prediction vectors.
       They do not automatically include uncertainty associated with retraining,
additional model-selection procedures, repeated assessment exposure, or future
industrial installations.
       An uncertainty interval containing zero does not establish that two methods are
equivalent. Likewise, a positive point estimate does not, by itself, establish a practically
meaningful or reproducible improvement.
       The interpretation of each comparison consequently retains its effect magnitude,
uncertainty procedure, assessment population, and scientific relevance.



4.9     Robustness Evaluation Design

The robustness of a multimodal prediction architecture concerns its behaviour when the
information available to its prediction components changes.
       Such changes can arise through unavailable observations, numerical corruption,
visual degradation, incorrect temporal correspondence, or differences in operating
conditions.
       These regimes affect the modelling problem through different mechanisms and
must therefore be evaluated separately.
       Clean-input assessment establishes predictive performance under reference



                                            115
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


observation conditions. Known modality absence evaluates behaviour when an input is
declared unavailable. Sensor corruption and visual degradation examine performance
under specified numerical measurement disturbances and changes to image quality,
respectively.
        Temporal misalignment concerns changes in the correspondence between
observations, while distribution shift concerns changes in the assessment distribution or
operating conditions. Complete input absence requires a defined system response when
no predictive branch remains available.
        The empirical studies test selected conditions from this framework. They do not
comprehensively evaluate temporal misalignment, naturally occurring sensing failures,
or broader deployment shifts.


4.9.1       Definition of Controlled Reliability Interventions

A robustness experiment requires an explicit description of how the input condition is
modified.
        The relevant intervention should identify the affected modality, the processing
stage at which the modification occurs, the severity of the disturbance, and the
observations associated with the assessment.
        When stochastic corruption is used, record the number of realisations and the
applicable randomisation procedure.
        The processing stage is consequential.
        For visual observations, corrupting image pixels before feature extraction
represents a different intervention from corrupting a cached visual embedding.
        For machine-sensor observations, modifying the original channels differs from
modifying aggregated statistics or prediction logits.
        Quality descriptors intended to represent the modified observation must be
handled consistently with the intervention. Otherwise, the gate may receive context
that does not correspond to the observation actually supplied to the predictor.
        The completed robustness comparisons therefore retain their specific corruption


                                           116
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


procedures and associated preparation stages.
        They are interpreted as evidence concerning those controlled interventions rather
than as comprehensive demonstrations of natural industrial reliability.


4.9.2     Known Modality Absence and Fallback

Known modality absence describes situations in which the prediction system receives
explicit information that one of its required observations is unavailable.
        An availability indicator can then determine which predictive branches remain
accessible.
        The original two-input REASSEMBLE gate assigns the surviving modality a
weight of one. Its robustness evaluator directly returns the surviving prediction
probability and uses the corresponding threshold.
        When both inputs are absent, the original implementation rejects the
observation rather than generating a prediction.
        This establishes a defined fallback policy.
        If several fusion mechanisms return the same surviving prediction, their
performance under that absence condition is determined by the surviving branch.
        Such a comparison does not establish that one learned gate recognises modality
absence more effectively or provides a better adaptive allocation.
        Training-time modality dropout can expose the model to selected missing-input
patterns, but its effectiveness must be assessed separately.
        The ability to continue prediction using an available branch and the ability to
learn a useful recovery mechanism are therefore distinct scientific questions.


4.9.3     Degraded but Available Observations

A degraded observation can remain available while containing less useful or misleading
information.
        Unlike known modality absence, this condition does not necessarily give the
prediction system an explicit indicator of which observation should receive reduced

                                            117
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


reliance.
         Adaptive fusion mechanisms aim to address this problem by using available
context or quality information to modify the relative contribution of the prediction
branches.
         However, a change in the learned allocation does not, by itself, establish that the
resulting prediction improves.
         The relevant scientific comparison evaluates the adaptive mechanism against
appropriate static fusion and unimodal references under the same degradation condition.
         Where one modality remains intact, its prediction provides an important
reference for assessing whether fusion with a corrupted companion improves or worsens
the resulting decision.
         The motivation for such conditional allocation is supported by quality-aware
fusion research, which examines the relationship between dynamic weights, unimodal
reliability, and predictive performance under specified assumptions (Q. Zhang et al.,
2023).
         The empirical investigation must nevertheless establish whether the available
descriptors provide useful information about the reliability of the particular industrial
or robotic prediction branches.


4.9.4       Training Exposure and Matched Comparisons

Training procedures can influence robustness independently of the fusion architecture.
         A model exposed to corrupted or incomplete observations during fitting may
develop different predictive behaviour from a model trained exclusively on intact
observations.
         If only an adaptive model receives degradation-related augmentation, its
performance improvement cannot be attributed exclusively to the adaptive mechanism.
         Comparisons between static and adaptive models with comparable corruption
exposure therefore provide a more informative basis for investigating the contribution of
adaptation.


                                             118
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


        Matching exposure addresses one important source of experimental difference.
However, differences in model capacity, optimisation procedures, feature representations,
and calibration may remain.
        The subsequent REASSEMBLE anchored-adjustment and quality-interaction
investigations retain these distinctions when evaluating clean and degraded predictions.
        The matched-exposure comparisons address a narrower scientific question than
the original comparisons between differently configured static and adaptive fusion
models.


4.9.5     Interpretation of Robustness Performance

Robustness evaluation requires consideration of both absolute degraded-input
performance and changes relative to the corresponding clean reference.
        A model may lose less performance relative to its clean baseline yet still perform
worse in absolute terms than another model under degradation.
        Likewise, an adaptive mechanism may visibly reduce its reliance on a corrupted
modality while still performing below the intact unimodal predictor.
        Neither a reduced relative degradation nor a change in learned weights
independently establishes improved reliability.
        An informative assessment therefore considers predictive performance under
intact inputs, absolute performance under the specified degradation, and changes
relative to the corresponding clean configuration.
        These findings must also be interpreted against the performance of available
unimodal references and appropriately matched static fusion mechanisms. Probability
quality and threshold-dependent decisions provide further evidence where evaluated.
        This framework distinguishes improvements in clean predictive effectiveness from
improvements under unreliable-input conditions.
        The robustness findings remain specific to the tested corruption operators,
severities, observation windows, and fitting procedures.
        The completed experiments do not establish comprehensive reliability under


                                           119
Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


unmeasured clock offsets, gradual calibration drift, simultaneous natural sensing faults,
or previously unseen industrial installations.



4.10       Reproducibility and Experiment Provenance

Reproducibility requires a documented relationship between the reported scientific
findings and the computational procedures that generated them.
       Supporting records include implementation sources, configurations, preprocessing
definitions, dataset manifests, model-fitting procedures, assessment populations, saved
predictions, and evaluation artefacts.
       Accessible code, recorded execution, and independently reproduced experimental
findings represent different levels of reproducibility assurance.
       These distinctions are particularly important in machine-learning research,
where model selection, software dependencies, preprocessing, and undocumented
configuration changes can affect the reported performance (Pineau et al., 2021).

4.10.2 Levels of Experimental Verification

The verification procedures distinguish between inspecting recorded research artefacts
and independently repeating the computational processes that generated them.
         Source inspection establishes which implementation and recorded computations
can be examined. Configuration inspection identifies model settings, preprocessing
choices, and fitting conditions. Execution records provide documentary evidence that a
particular experimental stage was completed.
         Saved-prediction verification goes further by recalculating performance measures
from retained model outputs.
         Repeated inference regenerates predictions from the original fitted model and its
inputs. Training reproduction fits the model again using the recorded procedure.
Independent empirical replication tests the findings with genuinely independent
experimental evidence.
         Verification during manuscript preparation includes inspection of archived source
code, configurations, and experimental results.
         Where retained predictions are available, selected metrics are independently
recalculated to check numerical consistency.
         Such checks provide evidence about the recorded predictions and metric
implementations. They do not constitute repeated model inference, reproduced training,
or independent empirical replication.

4.11        Computational Constraints and Exploratory

            Research Boundaries

The research programme was conducted under practical constraints concerning
computational resources, dataset availability, independent sample support, and the time
required for repeated fitting and evaluation.
         These conditions influenced the selection of cached representations, compact
prediction heads, limited hyperparameter grids, and bounded architectural follow-up
experiments.
         Frozen visual extraction reduces repeated encoder computation during grouped
prediction-head fitting. Compact statistical features provide a low-dimensional sensor
representation. Limited selection grids restrict the number of configurations examined
during model development.
         Such choices define the evaluated prediction procedures and their computational
scope.
         They do not establish that the selected architectures are computationally
optimal or that alternative configurations would necessarily perform worse.
         Similarly, a model evaluated under a restricted fitting budget cannot be declared

Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


intrinsically unsuitable solely because its performance is below that of a conventional
reference.
       Longer fitting, alternative representations, different preprocessing, or additional
supervision may influence the result. Their relevance must be established through the
corresponding comparisons.
       Unfinished experimental configurations also remain distinct from completed
configurations that produce negative findings.
       An unfinished configuration provides no empirical estimate of the performance it
might have achieved.


4.11.1 Computational Measurement Boundaries

Computational feasibility is part of evaluating industrial monitoring architectures.
       However, measurements of computational performance must identify which
operations are included.
       Feature extraction time, cached-representation processing, prediction-head
fitting, and end-to-end inference describe different computational workloads.
       The relevant measurement boundary may also depend on hardware, batch size,
data loading, preprocessing, synchronisation, and the availability of previously
extracted representations.
       A rapid forward-pass measurement does not independently establish real-time
monitoring capability because obtaining the required observation window can introduce
additional delay.
       Consequently, computational measurements are interpreted based on the stages
included in the recorded procedure.
       The available timings provide bounded evidence about the cost of the
corresponding components rather than establishing complete operational performance
in an industrial installation.

4.11.2 Exploratory Versus Confirmatory Interpretation

The research is predominantly exploratory and retrospective.
       The original scientific questions establish the purpose of the investigation, while
subsequent dataset qualification, architecture selection, and controlled follow-ups
respond to the observations and limitations identified during its development.
       Repeated examination of assessment populations, selecting follow-up
experiments after inspecting results, and later protocol refinements limit the
confirmatory interpretation of the resulting performance improvements.
       Although individual model fits can exclude their designated outer assessment
observations, this separation does not remove the influence of previous assessment
exposure on the broader research process.
       The completed experiments nevertheless provide useful comparative evidence
concerning the investigated mechanisms.
       Their scientific value arises from defining the evaluated prediction targets,
retaining credible reference models, documenting which conditions change between
comparisons, and identifying the boundaries of the supported conclusions.
       A future confirmatory investigation would require specifying the target
definition, observation horizon, cohort construction, model-selection procedure,
principal comparisons, corruption operators, evaluation metrics, and relevant decision
criteria before opening an independent assessment population.
       Such an investigation would provide a stronger basis for establishing whether the
observed architectural benefits persist beyond the current exploratory development
history.


4.11.3 Methodological Synthesis

The methods in this chapter explain what each comparison can—and cannot—show.
       Target validity and modality correspondence determine whether a proposed
prediction task can be meaningfully investigated. Representation definitions and fitting
procedures determine which information is retained and which modelling conditions

Chapter 4. Research Design and Shared Methodology


influence predictive performance.
       Unimodal predictors and simple fusion references provide the basis for assessing
incremental predictive value.
       Known modality absence and degraded but available observations require
different reliability interventions and comparison mechanisms.
       Grouped assessment, probability evaluation, class-specific metrics, and
uncertainty estimation establish the appropriate interpretation of the recorded
predictions.
       Reproducibility records and computational measurements further define the
evidence supporting the evaluated configurations.
       Taken together, these procedures apply requirements R1–R5 to the studies and
provide a consistent basis for answering the research questions.
       The requirements also identify where the available datasets and completed
experiments cannot support the intended comparison.
       The empirical studies therefore provide bounded evidence concerning the
suitability, predictive effectiveness, and reliability of the evaluated architectures under
their respective conditions.
       The following chapters apply this framework to the individual industrial and
robotic monitoring tasks. Each case study establishes its scientific motivation, describes
the corresponding investigation, reports the supported experimental findings, and
identifies the limitations governing their interpretation.
