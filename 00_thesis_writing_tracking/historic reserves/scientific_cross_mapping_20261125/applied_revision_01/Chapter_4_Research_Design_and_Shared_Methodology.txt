Chapter 4 — Research Design and Shared Methodology

Chapter 3 describes the technical components investigated in this thesis. Their utility depends on how they are implemented, trained, and tested. This chapter details the methods employed in the studies. It covers the selection of the datasets and targets, the pairing of the observations, the training and testing of the models and the various ways in which uncertainty and reliability were assessed.

4.1 Research Design and Relationship to the Research Questions

This thesis investigates multimodal methods for industrial monitoring, asking whether combining modalities improves prediction and how well the resulting methods perform. The research combines a literature review, dataset assessments, model development, comparative evaluations, and follow-up experiments. These experiments test whether particular representations and fusion techniques improve prediction under the conditions studied. The studies are primarily exploratory. The research questions motivated the work, while available data, preliminary results, time, and accessible methods shaped its direction.

The main comparisons are made within each study. Each representation or fusion method is tested against suitable reference models using the same target and assessment population.

The cross-dataset studies examine whether the methods remain useful across different tasks. Because the datasets differ in targets, observations, class balance, and evaluation procedures, their scores do not support a single cross-dataset ranking.

The PHM study constructs and predicts a continuous proxy for gear condition. The study addresses representation and fusion by comparing sensor representations and integrating sensor groups. REASSEMBLE is the main experimental setting for evaluating multimodal fusion architectures. Its labelled completed actions, paired visual data, and machine-sensor data support comparisons of representations, single-modality predictors, static fusion, and context-based methods.

MSM applies related methods to abnormal milling intervals. ViFailback extends the investigation to complete manipulation episodes assessed across held-out tasks. It additionally examines whether transferring a visual representation learned on REASSEMBLE influences the predictive usefulness of visual information. HANDOVER offers an additional comparison using video and force–torque data from labelled human–robot interaction trials. Its official assessment split supports evaluating single-modality predictors and fixed averaging. The completed assessment also studies controlled sensor noise, visual blur, known input loss, and class-specific decisions.

They also note differences in model size, calibration, and extra prediction steps where experiments allow.

These requirements provide a framework for interpreting the completed studies; they do not imply that every experiment followed the same protocol from the outset.

4.2 Dataset Selection Criteria and Research Chronology

Datasets were assessed for accessible observations and documentation, a clearly interpretable target, and the availability of native labels or independent reference measurements. The assessment also checked whether observations matched the prediction units, acquisition groups could be identified, outcome classes were represented, and the required processing and evaluation could be completed.

The first robotic welding proposal came before the PHM and REASSEMBLE studies. The PHM investigation had already started when the renewed Intel Welding audit introduced more restrictions on the planned independent assessment. REASSEMBLE then became the main experimental environment for comparing visual and machine-sensor representations and exploring different fusion architectures.

Later experiments looked into limitations found during the main studies. These included stronger sensor references, longer temporal-model training, different visual aggregation, encoder adaptation, context-conditioned fusion, and evaluation with external datasets. The October research-alignment record kept the original four-question hierarchy after considering a narrower quality-gate investigation.

Additional dataset investigations, including PADERBORN, REBOOT, and other feasibility studies, are documented in Appendix G. These investigations are distinguished from completed principal experiments according to whether they produced an admissible comparison, a methodological finding, or an unresolved feasibility decision.

The experiments were not planned as one continuous sequence. Several follow-up comparisons were designed in response to earlier results and therefore remain exploratory.

4.3 Intel Welding Feasibility Assessment and Scope Evolution

The Intel Welding feasibility investigation examined dataset structure, observation readability, timing and session information, duplicate observations, and the suitability of the available partitions. The associated foundation scripts inspect sample structure and assessment eligibility. Their recorded configuration turns off a final test because the proposed assessment population had already been examined during earlier context-related development. The renewed audit also identified acquisition sessions spanning the supplied partitions. The results prevented the proposed population from being used as an untouched, independent confirmatory assessment.

Prior examination of an assessment population does not mean that every observation was used to train a model. However, it does limit the extent to which a later evaluation can be treated as an independent test of a fixed architectural hypothesis. The retained dataset subset does not establish the entire original label mapping or a completed native multimodal welding classifier. Consequently, the earlier context-related baseline records provide evidence concerning feasibility and design of the assessment rather than demonstrating that the proposed Transformer-based fusion architecture failed to predict welding quality.

The project documentation attributes the transition toward PHM and REASSEMBLE to an agreement with the supervisor reported by the researcher. Success in adjacent industrial or robotic tasks does not establish successful welding-domain validation. An independent answer to the first welding application would require fresh welding observations, an appropriate weld-quality reference, and a reserved assessment population.

4.4 Dataset Admission and Preparation Checks

The sample manifest records each prediction unit, target source, physical or operational group, input path, observation boundaries, available modalities, and any reason for exclusion.

Visual preparation checks examine whether observations can be decoded, whether sufficient frames are available, whether the image geometry is compatible with the extraction procedure, and whether the relevant process is represented within the viewing window. An image may be technically readable yet provide no visible evidence of the target. Image quality and target visibility must therefore be assessed separately.

Sensor preparation checks examine channel ordering, physical units, timestamp consistency, missing observations, repeated measurements, and how interpolation or aggregation procedures are interpreted. Resampling is particularly important when measurements are collected at diverse temporal resolutions or when sequences are transformed into a common action-progress representation. These transformations must be recorded because they affect which properties of the source physical measurements remain available to the model.

Pairing checks whether observations from different sources belong to the same prediction unit. The mere presence of a video and a sensor file does not establish that they represent the same event or time interval.

Exclusions can influence the resulting class distribution and the difficulty of the assessment population. Removing corrupted observations or insufficiently represented failures may change the scientific interpretation of the comparison. Counts before and after exclusion, together with the reasons for exclusion, are consequently retained where supported by the experimental records. Quarantined observations retain their original target annotations. In MSM, timing-alias correction and grouping eligibility influence the admitted population. In ViFailback, duplicate or invalid telemetry records and video-readability failures affect the usable cohort.

These preparation checks establish technical eligibility. They do not independently validate semantic annotations, physical damage measurements, or the visibility of the relevant outcome within a camera observation. Failure to satisfy a particular criterion may restrict the proposed comparison without invalidating every possible investigation of the dataset. For example, insufficient participant information can prevent grouped learned-fusion fitting while still allowing an official fixed-model assessment. Similarly, the absence of an independent physical condition reference can restrict target interpretation while still permitting an investigation of proxy prediction.

These decisions operationalise R1 and establish the data-related conditions for the remaining research requirements.

4.5 Target Provenance, Prediction Units, and Available Inputs

A scientifically interpretable prediction task requires an explicit definition of what is predicted, how its reference value is obtained, and which observations are available at the intended prediction time. A model’s predictive performance alone cannot establish that its target accurately represents the underlying physical or operational phenomenon. This distinction is particularly relevant to the PHM investigation. Its image-derived candidate-area measure represents a provisional gear-condition proxy. Predicting the proxy from machine-sensor observations measures agreement with the constructed target. It does not independently establish the accuracy of physical wear estimation, tooth-damage measurement, or remaining-useful-life prediction.

In PHM, the principal prediction unit is a gear experiment run, and the target is a photograph-derived continuous condition proxy. REASSEMBLE predicts the recorded success or failure of completed robotic assembly actions. MSM predicts abnormal cutting within milling observation intervals. ViFailback predicts the recorded success or failure of complete manipulation episodes, while HANDOVER investigates binary outcomes associated with human–robot interaction trials. The MSM investigation retains its declared mapping of native milling labels. Alternative mappings are considered through sensitivity analyses because changing the interpretation of a native label also changes the prediction task.

Sampling visual frames across a complete robotic action can provide information about its concluding state. Similarly, sensor statistics calculated over an entire milling interval describe observations collected throughout that interval. These representations support retrospective assessment of condition or outcome. On their own, they do not show that a model can make a useful prediction before the operation is complete. Prospective monitoring would require a defined decision time, causal preprocessing, a specified lead-time measure, and target annotations appropriate to the intended prediction horizon.

The distinction also influences computational interpretation. A model may produce a prediction rapidly after receiving its input, while acquiring the required observation window introduces additional delay. The total time required for an operational prediction therefore includes both observation acquisition and the subsequent processing and inference stages.

Process identity, observation duration, time index, and other contextual variables can contain predictive information independently of the principal physical observations. For example, the extraction latency included in the PHM visual regression representation introduces a timing-related covariate alongside image-derived information. Similarly, an action-conditioned fusion mechanism may exploit differences in failure prevalence between action categories. Its predictive improvement would not automatically establish that it recognised a deterioration in physical sensor quality. Context-only references and constant-adjustment controls provide ways to investigate these alternative explanations where the corresponding comparisons are available.

4.6 Grouped Assessment, Data Splits, and Leakage Prevention

Industrial and robotic observations often exhibit dependence because several samples originate from the same physical experiment, recording, task, machine, participant, or acquisition session. Randomly partitioning individual observations may therefore place closely related samples into both development and assessment populations. Grouped assessment prevents overlap at the specified dependence-group level. It does not, by itself, establish independence at higher physical or operational levels.

The principal PHM sensor protocol holds out complete gear experiments, while REASSEMBLE groups actions by recording. MSM examines experiment-based and acquisition-date groupings, and ViFailback separates manipulation tasks. HANDOVER retains the published official trial partitions. The HANDOVER dataset publication describes its official participant-disjoint assessment. However, the available participant-to-trial correspondence does not permit independent reconstruction of that grouping or the construction of participant-disjoint inner training folds. For MSM, an acquisition date may include multiple tools or operators. Physical equipment may also recur across dates. Consequently, date-disjoint assessment does not establish generalisation to an entirely new machine, factory, or installation.

4.6.1 Training-Side Preprocessing and Model Selection

Within each outer assessment split, all data-dependent modelling operations must be considered part of model development. These operations include feature scaling and normalisation, imputation parameters estimated from data, feature selection, supervised representation learning, hyperparameter selection, probability calibration, decision-threshold selection, and learned fusion fitting.

Using cross-validation to select model configurations can produce optimistic error estimates if the same evaluation evidence is later used to report the selected performance (Varma & Simon, 2006). More generally, leakage must be assessed through the complete modelling procedure, not only through the final training operation (Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023). When a representation is derived using a fixed, generic pretrained encoder without target-specific supervision, its features may be cached before target-specific model fitting. However, any data-dependent normalisation, supervised adaptation, or feature selection must respect the corresponding permitted development population.

The principal PHM sensor protocol fits its normaliser separately within each training fold. However, a separate four-arm diagnostic runner fits its normaliser before entering the loop that changes the held-out gear experiment. In that diagnostic, observations from the held-out experiments therefore influence the fitted preprocessing transformation. The principal PHM experiment and the four-arm diagnostic must consequently remain separate when reporting their procedures and interpreting their results. A fully fold-isolated estimate for the four-arm diagnostic would require repeating the corresponding experiment under a revised preprocessing procedure. Such a repetition is not part of the completed evidence.

4.6.2 Out-of-Fold Predictions and Learned Fusion

Prediction-level stacking requires a second-stage training population containing the outputs of its first-stage predictors. Using predictions generated from observations already included in first-stage fitting can provide the stacker with an unrealistically favourable description of branch performance. Stacked generalisation therefore motivates constructing second-stage training inputs through cross-validated first-stage predictions (Ting & Witten, 1999).

For each outer assessment split, the outer training population is partitioned into grouped inner fitting and prediction subsets. Branch models generate predictions for observations whose corresponding recordings were excluded from their fitting. These predictions form an out-of-fold prediction bank used by subsequent training-side calibration, model-selection, and fusion-fitting procedures according to the relevant protocol. The final selected branch models and prediction-level combination are then used to generate predictions for the designated outer assessment recordings.

Within each outer split, the stacker's internal validation reuses an out-of-fold prediction bank generated from the outer training recordings. Although each predicted row originates from a branch that excluded its own recording, a branch used to generate a prediction for a stacker training row may have been fitted on recordings subsequently assigned to a stacker validation subset. The outer assessment recordings remain excluded from fitting. The shared prediction bank therefore limits claims of strict independence at the deeper meta-validation stage without, by itself, establishing leakage from the outer assessment population.

4.6.3 Assessment Cohorts and Repeated Exposure

The validity of a comparative experiment also depends on whether the evaluated predictors use identical assessment observations. The original REASSEMBLE assessment population contains 4,530 completed actions from 148 recordings, including 509 failures. Its five outer assessment partitions collectively cover this population.

A later comparison involving 753 actions uses one of these previously examined outer partitions. It therefore provides additional evidence for a bounded experimental comparison but does not constitute a new independent external holdout. An earlier summary identified this subset as containing 28 recordings. Reconciliation of the recorded sample and recording identifiers establishes that the population contains 26 recordings. The assessment identities, rather than the earlier summary count, define the population associated with the comparison.

Internal cohort evidence. E052.

Grouped assessment protects against specified forms of information overlap within individual model fits. It does not remove the consequences of repeatedly examining the same assessment population during exploratory research. When later configurations are selected in response to previously observed assessment results, those results influence the research development process even if their observations remain excluded from the training of each subsequent model. The REASSEMBLE follow-up investigations therefore provide informative controlled comparisons within an already examined population.

4.7 Model Fitting, Selection, Calibration, and Threshold Procedures

Each empirical comparison identifies its trainable components, initialisation procedure, preprocessing, candidate configurations, training budget, and relevant stopping or selection conditions. Several more extensive experiments completed only a subset of their originally planned configurations. Their interpretation is restricted to the comparisons for which the required fitting and assessment stages were completed. The prediction procedure distinguishes four operations: fitting model parameters, selecting a model, calibrating probabilities, and selecting a decision threshold.

Model fitting estimates the parameters of the selected prediction architecture using its permitted training observations. Model selection subsequently determines which representation, hyperparameter configuration, or fitting procedure is retained for assessment. Equating the number of training epochs does not necessarily mean equivalent optimisation exposure if the configurations differ in batch size, available observations, early stopping, initialisation, or trainable components. Similarly, a pretrained frozen representation and a scratch-trained temporal encoder receive different forms of prior information and allocate computational resources differently.

Calibration methods use development-side predictions and corresponding outcomes to adjust the numerical interpretation of prediction scores. Validation-based calibration can improve probability quality under appropriate conditions, but its effectiveness must be assessed rather than assumed (Guo et al., 2017). The principal REASSEMBLE branch calibrators use sigmoid logistic maps fitted to selected inner out-of-fold prediction scores.

F2 introduces a fitted logistic map with a free intercept and separate branch coefficients. It does not include an additional post-stacker calibration stage in the recorded core implementation. The original context-conditioned gates combine branch logits through normalised weights and apply a sigmoid to the resulting weighted value. They do not include a free final intercept or an additional post-gate calibrator.

A strictly increasing transformation preserves the ordering of observations within a fixed assessment population. Consequently, it preserves ranking-based metrics such as average precision and AUROC within that population, subject to numerical precision and tie handling. When a sensor probability is transformed through a logistic function with a positive sensor coefficient, its ordering remains unchanged within the corresponding fold. However, independently fitted transformations can differ between assessment folds. When predictions from different folds are pooled, these transformations may change the relative ordering of observations originating from different folds, even though the ordering within each fold remains unchanged. Consequently, pooled average precision may change even when the ranking within every individual fold remains unchanged.

A comparison can involve both additional information from another prediction branch and an additional fitted transformation of the existing score. Within-fold discrimination, pooled ranking, and probability calibration therefore represent distinct effects. A matched sensor-only second-stage predictor provides an additional control for investigating the contribution of the fitted transformation. Where such a separately fitted control is unavailable, arithmetic analysis of the retained model can provide narrower evidence about the contribution of particular terms without resolving the complete attribution problem. REASSEMBLE’s attribution of the full pooled fusion improvement is restricted by the absence of an independently refitted sensor-only second-stage control. The corresponding REASSEMBLE saved-prediction investigation is presented in Chapter 6.

Observations with prediction scores at or above the selected threshold are classified as positive, while those below the threshold are classified as negative. The original implementation examines a threshold grid ranging from 0.01 to 0.99 using permitted training-side predictions and a specified selection objective, such as balanced accuracy. A threshold selected in this way belongs to the experimental evaluation protocol. It is not necessarily an operationally optimal industrial alarm threshold. Industrial decision-making may assign different costs to missed failures, false alarms, interruptions, or unnecessary maintenance. Establishing an operationally appropriate threshold would require information about these consequences and the intended monitoring application.

4.8 Metrics and Uncertainty Estimation

Regression error, classification discrimination, probability quality, and threshold-dependent decisions describe different properties of a prediction model. Their interpretation also depends on the target definition, class distribution, assessment population, and aggregation procedure.

4.8.1 Regression, Classification, and Probability Metrics

Mean absolute error describes the average absolute difference between predicted and observed target values. Root mean squared error calculates the square root of the average squared prediction error. Both measures retain the units of the prediction target. The coefficient of determination compares prediction error against the variation of the observed target values. It may become negative when the fitted predictor performs poorly relative to its reference denominator and can be unstable when the variation within an assessment group is limited. Tooth-level and run-level regression measurements are reported separately because they represent different prediction units and dependence structures.

Accuracy describes the proportion of correctly classified observations. Precision measures the proportion of positive predictions corresponding to actual positive outcomes. Recall measures the proportion of actual positives identified by the classifier. Specificity measures the proportion of negative observations correctly classified, while balanced accuracy gives equal importance to sensitivity and specificity. F1 combines precision and recall through their harmonic mean.

Ranking-based measures instead examine how well the model orders positive and negative observations across possible thresholds. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, AUROC, evaluates discrimination between positive and negative observations. Average precision summarises the precision–recall relationship and provides a useful measure for classification tasks involving imbalanced outcomes. Precision–recall analysis can reveal aspects of predictive performance that are less apparent from ROC-based interpretation when one outcome class is relatively uncommon (Saito & Rehmsmeier, 2015).

The metrics available for each study depend on its target, prediction outputs, and completed evaluation protocol. Their inclusion in the shared methodological framework does not imply that every metric was calculated for every experiment.

The principal classification ranking metric is average precision, calculated using the average_precision_score implementation. Average precision summarises the precision–recall relationship by weighting precision values by their corresponding increments in recall. This formulation is distinct from estimating the area under a precision–recall curve through trapezoidal numerical integration.

Average precision depends on the prevalence of the designated positive class. If a binary predictor assigns the same score to every observation, its average precision equals the positive-class prevalence in the assessment population. A high failure average precision may provide limited evidence of discrimination when failure already represents the majority of the evaluated observations. This principle is particularly relevant to the ViFailback assessment, where failure constitutes more than 85% of the principal eligible population. Reporting only failure average precision can therefore obscure the model’s ability to identify successful outcomes.

Where both outcomes are relevant, class-specific average precision can be calculated separately for success and failure. Their binary macro average is obtained by calculating the arithmetic mean of these two class-specific values. For a constant-score binary predictor, the both-class macro average precision reference equals 0.5 when both outcome classes are present. REASSEMBLE principally reports failure average precision. MSM reports abnormal-cutting average precision. ViFailback additionally reports both-class macro average precision. These are distinct evaluation quantities and must not be interpreted as directly comparable numerical measures of architectural performance across the datasets.

The Brier score measures the average squared difference between predicted probabilities and observed binary outcomes. Proper scoring rules provide a principled basis for assessing probability forecasts rather than only their ranking or thresholded decisions (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007).

Expected calibration error provides a complementary description based on discrepancies between predicted confidence and observed outcome frequencies across probability bins. Its interpretation depends on the number and construction of bins, the definition of confidence, and the available observations within each bin. A classifier with strong ranking performance may still assign poorly calibrated probabilities. Conversely, a model with improved probability error may not provide better discrimination. Probability calibration can also change under altered input conditions. Evidence from clean-data calibration must therefore be interpreted separately from probability quality under degradation or distribution shift (Ovadia et al., 2019).

4.8.2 Aggregation and Uncertainty

Mean-fold average precision is calculated by obtaining the average precision for each assessment fold and subsequently averaging those values. Pooled average precision instead combines the corresponding held-out prediction vectors and calculates average precision over the combined population. Because average precision is nonlinear, these aggregation procedures generally produce different numerical results.

The original REASSEMBLE primary comparison reports pooled failure average precision across its held-out recording partitions. MSM reports abnormal-cutting average precision averaged over seed-specific pooled predictions. Reported seed variation and fold variation must also remain separate. Different random initialisations or fitting seeds represent another source of experimental variation rather than additional independently acquired assessment observations.

For two prediction models evaluated on the same observations, a paired comparison can be obtained by subtracting the average precision of the reference model from that of the investigated model. The paired construction retains the common observations and corresponding outcomes for the two models. The uncertainty analyses use cluster-based bootstrap procedures where supported by the retained experimental records. A cluster bootstrap resamples the specified dependence groups with replacement, rather than resampling individual observations independently. It then recalculates the paired metric difference for each resampled population.

The core REASSEMBLE reports use 2,000 recording-level bootstrap replicates. A PHM report uses 10,000 run-cluster bootstrap replicates over 20 runs from three gear experiments. The latter resampling procedure requires particular care because runs from the same gear experiment may retain higher-level dependence. Run-level resampling does not automatically capture all dependence between observations originating from the same physical experiment. Increasing the number of bootstrap replicates reduces the numerical variability of the estimated resampling distribution. It does not increase the number of independently acquired physical experiments. Similarly, the MSM date-cluster intervals rely on a particularly limited population of five acquisition dates. Their interpretation remains conditional on those groups and the recorded fitted models.

Cross-validation folds also share training observations. They should therefore not be treated as independent experimental replications when calculating uncertainty. Bengio and Grandvalet (2004) establish the limitations of universally unbiased variance estimation for k-fold cross-validation, supporting caution when constructing uncertainty estimates from overlapping training populations. The retained bootstrap intervals characterise uncertainty under the selected resampling unit and fitted prediction vectors. They do not automatically include uncertainty associated with retraining, additional model-selection procedures, repeated assessment exposure, or future industrial installations. An uncertainty interval containing zero does not establish that two methods are equivalent. Likewise, a positive point estimate does not, by itself, establish a practically meaningful or reproducible improvement.

4.9 Robustness Evaluation Design

Clean-input assessment establishes predictive performance under reference observation conditions. Known modality absence evaluates behaviour when an input is declared unavailable. Sensor corruption and visual degradation examine performance under specified numerical measurement disturbances and changes to image quality, respectively. Temporal misalignment concerns changes in the correspondence between observations, while distribution shift concerns changes in the assessment distribution or operating conditions. Complete input absence requires a defined system response when no predictive branch remains available. The empirical studies test selected conditions from this framework. They do not comprehensively evaluate temporal misalignment, naturally occurring sensing failures, or broader deployment shifts.

4.9.1 Controlled Interventions, Absence, and Degradation

A robustness experiment requires an explicit description of how the input condition is modified. The relevant intervention should identify the affected modality, the processing stage at which the modification occurs, the severity of the disturbance, and the observations associated with the assessment. When stochastic corruption is used, record the number of realisations and the applicable randomisation procedure. For visual observations, corrupting image pixels before feature extraction represents a different intervention from corrupting a cached visual embedding. For machine-sensor observations, modifying the original channels differs from modifying aggregated statistics or prediction logits. Quality descriptors intended to represent the modified observation must be handled consistently with the intervention. Otherwise, the gate may receive context that does not correspond to the observation actually supplied to the predictor.

The original two-input REASSEMBLE gate assigns the surviving modality a weight of one. Its robustness evaluator directly returns the surviving prediction probability and uses the corresponding threshold. When both inputs are absent, the original implementation rejects the observation rather than generating a prediction. If several fusion mechanisms return the same surviving prediction, their performance under that absence condition is determined by the surviving branch. Such a comparison does not establish that one learned gate recognises modality absence more effectively or provides a better adaptive allocation. Training-time modality dropout can expose the model to selected missing-input patterns, but its effectiveness must be assessed separately.

A degraded observation can remain available while containing less useful or misleading information. Unlike known modality absence, this condition does not necessarily give the prediction system an explicit indicator of which observation should receive reduced reliance. The relevant scientific comparison evaluates the adaptive mechanism against appropriate static fusion and unimodal references under the same degradation condition. Where one modality remains intact, its prediction provides an important reference for assessing whether fusion with a corrupted companion improves or worsens the resulting decision.

The motivation for such conditional allocation is supported by quality-aware fusion research, which examines the relationship between dynamic weights, unimodal reliability, and predictive performance under specified assumptions (Q. Zhang et al., 2023). The empirical investigation must nevertheless establish whether the available descriptors provide useful information about the reliability of the particular industrial or robotic prediction branches.

4.9.2 Matched Exposure and Robustness Interpretation

A model exposed to corrupted or incomplete observations during fitting may develop different predictive behaviour from a model trained exclusively on intact observations. If only an adaptive model receives degradation-related augmentation, its performance improvement cannot be attributed exclusively to the adaptive mechanism. Comparisons between static and adaptive models with comparable corruption exposure therefore provide a more informative basis for investigating the contribution of adaptation. Matching exposure addresses one important source of experimental difference. However, differences in model capacity, optimisation procedures, feature representations, and calibration may remain. The subsequent REASSEMBLE anchored-adjustment and quality-interaction investigations retain these distinctions when evaluating clean and degraded predictions. The matched-exposure comparisons address a narrower scientific question than the original comparisons between differently configured static and adaptive fusion models.

Robustness evaluation requires consideration of both absolute degraded-input performance and changes relative to the corresponding clean reference. A model may lose less performance relative to its clean baseline yet still perform worse in absolute terms than another model under degradation. Likewise, an adaptive mechanism may visibly reduce its reliance on a corrupted modality while still performing below the intact unimodal predictor. These findings must also be interpreted against the performance of available unimodal references and appropriately matched static fusion mechanisms. Probability quality and threshold-dependent decisions provide further evidence where evaluated. The robustness findings remain specific to the tested corruption operators, severities, observation windows, and fitting procedures. The completed experiments do not establish comprehensive reliability under unmeasured clock offsets, gradual calibration drift, simultaneous natural sensing faults, or previously unseen industrial installations.

4.10 Reproducibility and Experiment Provenance

Supporting records include implementation sources, configurations, preprocessing definitions, dataset manifests, model-fitting procedures, assessment populations, saved predictions, and evaluation artefacts. Accessible code, recorded execution, and independently reproduced experimental findings represent different levels of reproducibility assurance. These distinctions are particularly important in machine-learning research, where model selection, software dependencies, preprocessing, and undocumented configuration changes can affect the reported performance (Pineau et al., 2021).

The principal REASSEMBLE visual branch loads the pretrained RT-DETR-R18vd checkpoint through the Hugging Face Transformers library and selects its ResNet18-vd convolutional backbone. The Hugging Face Transformers library also supplies the PatchTST sensor encoder, which is initialised from scratch for the investigated prediction task (Wolf et al., 2020). PyTorch supports the neural-network implementation and fitting procedures, with AdamW used for the relevant neural prediction components (Paszke et al., 2019). Scikit-learn provides the grouped stratified splitting procedures, logistic regression references, and corresponding evaluation functionality (Pedregosa et al., 2011). The PHM visual regression implementation uses the Ultralytics RT-DETR-L prediction path and extracts intermediate feature maps for its downstream regression representation (Ultralytics, 2023).

For historical experiments, the available configuration and execution records identify the documented prediction procedure. Missing production scripts, incomplete historical configurations, or unavailable artefacts limit independent verification.

4.11 Computational Constraints and Exploratory Research Boundaries

The research programme was conducted under practical constraints concerning computational resources, dataset availability, independent sample support, and the time required for repeated fitting and evaluation. These conditions influenced the selection of cached representations, compact prediction heads, limited hyperparameter grids, and bounded architectural follow-up experiments. Frozen visual extraction reduces repeated encoder computation during grouped prediction-head fitting. Compact statistical features provide a low-dimensional sensor representation. Limited selection grids restrict the number of configurations examined during model development. Such choices define the evaluated prediction procedures and their computational scope. They do not establish that the selected architectures are computationally optimal or that alternative configurations would necessarily perform worse.

Similarly, a model evaluated under a restricted fitting budget cannot be declared intrinsically unsuitable solely because its performance is below that of a conventional reference. Longer fitting, alternative representations, different preprocessing, or additional supervision may influence the result. Their relevance must be established through the corresponding comparisons. Unfinished experimental configurations also remain distinct from completed configurations that produce negative findings. An unfinished configuration provides no empirical estimate of the performance it might have achieved.

Feature extraction time, cached-representation processing, prediction-head fitting, and end-to-end inference describe different computational workloads. The relevant measurement boundary may also depend on hardware, batch size, data loading, preprocessing, synchronisation, and the availability of previously extracted representations. The available timings provide bounded evidence about the cost of the corresponding components rather than establishing complete operational performance in an industrial installation.

Repeated examination of assessment populations, selecting follow-up experiments after inspecting results, and later protocol refinements limit the confirmatory interpretation of the resulting performance improvements. Although individual model fits can exclude their designated outer assessment observations, this separation does not remove the influence of previous assessment exposure on the broader research process. A future confirmatory investigation would require specifying the target definition, observation horizon, cohort construction, model-selection procedure, principal comparisons, corruption operators, evaluation metrics, and relevant decision criteria before opening an independent assessment population.

The following chapters apply this framework to the individual industrial and robotic monitoring tasks. Each case study establishes its scientific motivation, describes the corresponding investigation, reports the supported experimental findings, and identifies the limitations governing their interpretation.
