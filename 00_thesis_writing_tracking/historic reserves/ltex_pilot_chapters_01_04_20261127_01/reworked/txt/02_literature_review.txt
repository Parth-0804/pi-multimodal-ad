Critical Literature Review

Multimodal Learning in Industrial and Robotic Monitoring

Industrial monitoring covers tasks such as fault detection, checking a system's physical condition, assessing manufacturing quality, and confirming that a robot has completed its task. The traditional approach uses single sensor readings and engineered features to characterise the system's behaviour. Learned representations take this further by using data from images, sound and sensor sequences. Multimodal learning examines how information from different modalities can be combined to support a common prediction task (Baltrušaitis et al., 2019; Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka et al., 2024b).

The literature touches on several related areas of research: traditional feature engineering, the use of pretrained representations, modelling over time, feature combination, prediction fusion, and adaptive methods (where the method can adapt to the context). They differ in the information they require, their computational cost, the level of supervision they need, and their sensitivity to input quality.

Industrial and robotic monitoring provides a platform on which to compare these approaches as the process can be monitored via a number of different modalities (cameras, acoustic sensors, machine sensors, etc.). Cameras provide a view of the visible process, whereas the acoustic and machine sensors provide information which is not visible. The benefit of combining data depends heavily on what each modality retains, the specific prediction you're trying to make, and the system's test conditions (W. Wang et al., 2020; Hua et al., 2026; Goodarzi et al., 2023). Simply increasing the amount of data does not necessarily lead to better predictions.

Work on welding using visual and acoustic data has shown that multimodal integration can improve prediction performance, but also that, in some cases, adding another modality provides no benefit at all (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026). These findings raise a more general architectural question across industrial and robotic monitoring tasks. Under what conditions does a second observation supply information about system condition or task outcome that the strongest single input misses?

The review examines four questions. Which representations retain the information needed for the prediction? What does combining inputs add? How does that benefit change when an input is missing or degraded? Which assessment conditions make the resulting comparison credible?

Review Scope and Search Methodology

The review examines industrial fusion claims through the methodologies, comparison tables, and experimental results of the papers. It focuses on the link between the proposed architecture, the available physical observations, the prediction target, and the assessment protocol.

A research gap is defined as a comparison that the existing body of scientific evidence does not address for a given target, input set and assessment population. The ability to identify such a gap depends on the search strategy and the amount of published literature available.

Research Themes and Search Questions

The representation theme concerns what is being observed, what transformations are being applied, and whether the representation retains the information relevant to the task. The fusion theme is about whether adding further inputs improves performance on a standard prediction task compared with a set of plausible single-input references.

The robustness theme looks at which absence, corruption, and distribution-change regimes were assessed. The evaluation theme looks at assessment independence, thresholds, uncertainty, resource measurement and reproducibility.

These themes intentionally include negative and inconclusive findings. Comparisons where an additional modality yields minimal or no benefit can provide valuable insights into representation suitability, redundancy, and the limitations of the evaluated fusion procedure.

The review clearly distinguishes between two objectives that are often confused. Training with multiple modalities to deploy a single-input model is a privileged training scenario. Combining multiple available inputs at deployment is a multimodal inference problem. Cross-modal distillation and reconstruction link the two objectives, but the acquisition assumptions are very different.

The taxonomy of Baltrušaitis et al. (2019) is the organising terminology. The industrial claims are then assessed against the original studies rather than being entirely inferred from that survey.

Sources, Search Terms, and Search Period

The literature review was carried out as part of the project. The initial search was focused on the period 2016 to 2026 with a selection of earlier methodological papers also included. Of the 120 papers identified, 39 met the inclusion criteria and 37 were fully appraised.

A subsequent expansion identified 57 distinct full-text files after duplicate removal and version verification. The comparison register comprises 94 unique work records, representing a curated selection rather than an estimate of the entire research field.

Searches were conducted pairing industrial monitoring and manufacturing terms with fusion, stage, attention, missing inputs, noise and domain shift. Dataset searches included MVTec AD, MVTec 3D-AD, Eyecandies, bearing datasets and machine sound benchmarks.

Publisher and repository searches facilitated publication retrieval. Crossref supplied metadata for identity verification (Crossref, 2020), while OpenAlex supported citation tracing (Priem et al., 2022). Zotero was employed to link bibliographic records with PDFs and appraisal notes (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, 2026).

Appendix A differentiates the initial screening population from the subsequent full-text appraisal. The seminal stacked generalisation paper by Ting & Witten (1999) was included to support the discussion of prediction-level fusion methodology, representing a separate addition to the historical full-text appraisal count.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Direct industrial work focuses on faults, physical condition, manufacturing quality, inspection, and the outcome of a robotic task. Dataset papers and legitimate unimodal methods are also relevant, as fusion depends on a well-defined prediction target and a suitable reference.

Transferable work is explicitly identified. Work on multimodal optimisation failure modes, missing-input mechanisms, uncertainty weighting, and evaluation theory can all be valuable in designing industrial architectures, even if they do not amount to industrial validation (W. Wang et al., 2020; M. Ma et al., 2022; S. Lee et al., 2026; Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004).

A publication may be included in the register as background or as a methodological contribution. Simulated robot regression, RGB-geometry inspection, physical camera-telemetry monitoring and single-camera feature-level fusion are different experimental settings (Altinses & Schwung, 2023; Deng et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b; Z. Zhang et al., 2024).

Inaccessible evidence is not taken to be irrelevant, and missing reporting does not establish that a particular operation was not performed. The language used by the individual authors is interpreted in the context of the actual acquisition and evaluation procedure to determine whether the study yields multimodal observations, inference-time robustness evidence, or an equivalent unimodal reference.

Evidence Extraction and Critical Appraisal

Each appraisal records the source identity and version inspected, the prediction target and unit, the physical inputs, the transformations, the fusion method, the comparison models, the partitioning, the training access, and the reliability interventions.

Experimental results are linked to pages, tables and figures. Where possible, decisive PDF tables have been checked, as text extraction may obscure input columns, numerical values, or footnotes.

The appraisal records both controlled and varying factors. A common assessment cohort supports comparisons between fitted pipelines. To isolate fusion's contribution, however, differences in encoders, supervision, augmentation, and model capacity must also be considered.

Input subset models, complete inference-time absence, corrupted present data and target domain adaptation are all recorded separately.

The comparison matrix in Appendix A contains the work-level evidence. The compact classification codes are interpreted alongside the relevant protocol descriptions and source locators.

Limitations of the Review

The papers reviewed are by no means exhaustive and are not statistically independent. Several inspection studies use the same MVTec 3D-AD or Eyecandies dataset, so improvements on those benchmarks are not necessarily indicative of improvements on independent industrial installations. The preprints are also version-specific, and some publication details are still provisional.

When relatively few studies examine a particular condition, the body of evidence is such that any conclusions about that condition are necessarily weak.

The main MVTec 3D-AD table gives 249 normal and 948 anomalous test samples (total 1197) (Bergmann et al., 2022, Table 1). The welding penetration paper reports inconsistent acquisition and training numbers (Hua et al., 2026, pp. 6708, 6711), and the light inspection paper reports different NwfDD values in its main table and its ablation table (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, Tables IV, VI–VII).

These inconsistencies reduce the precision of the comparisons. The studies remain useful when their methods and reported findings are interpreted within clearly stated limits.

Industrial Monitoring Tasks and Supervision

Different monitoring tasks rely on different assumptions about training data, available observations, and acceptable levels of prediction error. These assumptions must be understood prior to comparing representation learning and fusion architectures.

The definition of the prediction target is crucial, as performance on one industrial task cannot be considered valid evidence of performance on another.

Anomaly Detection, Defect Classification, and Failure Classification

Normal-only anomaly detection learns a reference for nominal observations. Supervised defect classification, by contrast, uses labelled fault or quality classes. MVTec AD and MVTec 3D-AD are examples of the normal-only approach, whereas welding penetration recognition is an example of supervised classification (Bergmann et al., 2019; Bergmann et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2026).

HANDOVER defines five completed-interaction outcome classes: success, no approach, no grasp, no release and object drop (Thoduka et al., 2024b). A task failure in the operational domain doesn't necessarily mean that some physical damage has occurred or that we can identify the root cause of that damage.

Supervision is thus an important aspect of the evaluation. The weakly supervised inspection study brings the MVTec data back in, as training requires image-level anomalous labels, which of course changes the normal-only setting (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, p. 6659).

ZSG-IAD trains on reports and masks before evaluating on held-out categories or datasets. The zero-shot terminology refers to that assessment boundary rather than to the complete absence of anomaly supervised training (Q. Chen et al., 2026, pp. 6, 8–9).

These differences establish the need to compare architectures against their respective target definitions and under their respective supervision conditions.

Condition Estimation and Regression

Continuous prediction calls for attention to measurement units, reference observations, and the horizon at which the target is defined. Remaining useful life is a very different prediction problem from instantaneous defect probability.

Al-Dulaimi et al. (2019) look at a hybrid CNN-LSTM on simulated C-MAPSS trajectories using RMSE and an asymmetric prognostic score. Both branches of their model process the same set of selected telemetry channels. The study therefore provides evidence of complementary representations of the sensor data rather than any physical camera/sensor complementarity.

The synthetic robot benchmarks of Altinses and Schwung (2025) examine cross-modal prediction, reconstruction, and downstream regression. They show the importance of relationships between representations, though errors on different reconstructed outputs do not necessarily tell you which modality is more useful for a given common prediction task.

This issue is particularly relevant to proxy supervision. A model may accurately predict a derived condition index, yet the relationship between that index and actual physical deterioration may remain uncertain. A defensible regression comparison must therefore define the target construction, measurement scale, fitted pre-processing, and common assessment observations before interpreting numerical improvement as indicative of enhanced condition monitoring.

Dataset-Provided Labels, Derived Targets, and Proxy Supervision

Reference labels have very different evidential roles. Post-build CT provides flaw localisation for the voxel-level monitoring study by Petrich et al. (2021). Microscopy and density assessments are used to define quality classes in J. Li et al. (2022). Operando X-ray imaging is used to define process-state labels in Pandiyan et al. (2022).

These studies show how an independent instrument can establish or validate a prediction target without necessarily being available at the time of model inference. It is vital to distinguish between supervision and predictive inputs when describing the information available to a monitoring architecture.

For derived labels, predictive performance must be distinguished from independent physical validation. If the target is computed from the same measurements supplied to the model, then a low prediction error may reflect the model recovering that calculation rather than identifying an independent physical state.

At the regional level, a single quality label may be assigned to many adjacent observations. Their spatial or temporal dependence affects the choice of an appropriate assessment unit.

Native dataset annotations, independent reference measurements and analytical proxies support different conclusions about physical state and operational outcome.

Retrospective Assessment and Online Monitoring

The observation horizon is what determines when the model's forecast becomes available. A predictor assessed after the full observation period has elapsed is effectively given a retrospective assessment unless its inputs are restricted to an earlier decision time.

HANDOVER is very clear that its baselines classify outcomes post-trial, with causal online prediction still being work in progress (Thoduka et al., 2024b, p. 7, Section VI). A post-pass photo, a layer summary and the completed action all provide different information horizons from prospective monitoring (X. Zhang et al., 2026; Petrich et al., 2021).

Retrospective classification is still useful for assessing representations and fusion techniques, though its use in an operational context differs from that of an early warning system.

Computational delay must also be distinguished from observation time. DMAW uses 20 frames at 37 fps and reports a much shorter forward-pass latency. As a result, approximately half a second of observation is distinct from computational delay (Fan et al., 2026, pp. 6–7).

Similarly, simply varying the input duration of a CNN–LSTM does not, by itself, mean that a warning can be generated before a defect occurs (Pandiyan et al., 2022). Active monitoring requires a defined information cut-off, acquisition delay and end-to-end prediction workload.

Multimodal Data in Industrial Applications

Complementarity is determined by the additional task-relevant information provided by an observation or representation, rather than by differences in tensor shape or the number of acquisition sources.

Different representations of the same physical quantity can contain complementary information. Likewise, physically distinct sensors can provide redundant or only very weakly relevant information for a given prediction target.

The review therefore distinguishes between physical acquisition channels and derived representations and considers ordinary pairing separately from cases requiring stronger correspondence, such as cross-modal reconstruction or monitoring of a localised process.

Visual and Video Observations

RGB images, thermal images, process videos and geometric observations all have different acquisition assumptions. Depth, depth-derived point clouds, and estimated surface normals can all be multiple views of the same geometric source.

GPAD combines RGB-depth features with point cloud priors (M. Li et al., 2026, pp. 4–7). This is an example of integration across representations that do not necessarily require three separate physical acquisition sources.

RGB-geometry benchmarks describe appearance and shape rather than controller dynamics or motor current (Bergmann et al., 2022; Bonfiglioli et al., 2023).

Physical camera-plus-measurement precedents include thermal imaging with vibration, welding images with sound, tool images with acoustic emission, and robot video with force/torque and gripper state (Huo et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2026; X. Zhang et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b).

Their prediction units range from static inspection through to process windows and full trials. In the tool wear study, the camera is used post-cutting pass, with the preceding acoustic emission capturing the active cutting (X. Zhang et al., 2026, pp. 19–22).

The representation of visual observations also influences the information available. Both pooled frame features and video Transformers process multiple images, but they differ in how they treat temporal relationships.

These distinctions between them lead us to explicitly consider visual acquisition, observation windows, and aggregation within multimodal monitoring architectures.

Sensor Time Series and Process Measurements

Machine measurements can vary enormously in their physical origin and in their semantic meaning. An accelerometer measures vibration, a pressure sensor measures fluid behaviour, and controller logs can contain commands, estimates, or tracking errors rather than a direct measure of a physical quantity.

The harmonic-drive study combines high-frequency vibration with servo information, whereas PGMTKD transfers pressure-vibration teacher knowledge to a pressure-only student (Yang et al., 2024; C. Li et al., 2025).

Both papers address incomplete information, but their sensor availability and deployment reasons differ.

Representations can also inflate the apparent number of modalities without including any new measurements. Kullu and Cinar (2022) combine current and vibration using both raw and STFT representations. Wan et al. (2024) derive statistical 'vibration' features from two acoustic sensors without acquiring an accelerometer channel at all. C. Wang et al. (2025) combine source domain features with the decisions from the vibration data.

These studies are useful as architectural comparators, as they examine different information representations. Their results should, of course, be treated as distinct from comparisons that incorporate additional physical acquisition sources.

An acquisition inventory and a description of the retained representations are required prior to attributing any predictive gain to modality diversity.

Audio Observations

Audio can pick up machine/process events that are only very weakly visible. The relationship to fault state depends heavily on background conditions, mic location, and sync.

MIMII deliberately incorporates factory background recordings at a range of signal-to-noise ratios. Its fan baseline AUC drops from .94 at +6 dB to .70 at -6 dB (Purohit et al., 2019, Table 3).

ToyADMOS provides miniature machine recordings and an explicit noise-mixing protocol, making it a useful reference for acoustic monitoring (Koizumi et al., 2019).

In process monitoring, airborne acoustic signals can be paired with either a melt pool camera or a photodiode. Signal-to-image encoding in the study by J. Li et al. (2022) is an acoustic-optical-signal experiment, and Xie et al. (2025) use actual camera images.

DCASE2020 Task2 provides audio-only baselines under an evaluation protocol that differs from that of later domain shift challenges (Koizumi et al., 2020).

Together, these studies motivate acoustic representations as candidates for industrial multimodal monitoring. Their utility will depend on the availability of appropriate recordings, well-defined targets, and correspondence with other observations.

Synchronisation, Correspondence, and Modality Availability

Fusion requires observations that refer to a compatible event, region or time interval. Predicting one modality from another or combining aligned spatial discrepancy maps requires stronger correspondence.

RGB-point cloud feature mapping uses registered benchmark geometry. Process monitoring may instead require time synchronisation and projection onto a common spatial grid (Costanzino et al., 2024; Petrich et al., 2021).

In the inspected author manuscript, the digital twin study maps sensor features and prediction outputs to robot TCP positions. These positions provide spatial registration; they are not among the 27 listed sensor features (L. Chen et al., 2023).

Modality availability has several levels. A file can exist without having a usable paired observation for a given prediction unit. A file can have a successfully paired observation which is still degraded.

The acoustic-photodiode study explicitly lacks modality registration, which limits claims about correspondence between its two encoded streams (J. Li et al., 2022).

For camera and machine sensor monitoring, we can have an explicit pairing rule that picks up unmatched events even if both underlying files are present. Modality completeness should therefore be assessed on a prediction-unit basis rather than inferred from the presence or absence of the files.

Representation Learning for Industrial Monitoring

The choice of representation depends on what an industrial prediction task requires.

The literature reviewed supports both engineered and learned representations. Which is more appropriate will depend on the level of supervision, the level of preprocessing, the level of independent sample support, the information required, and the resources available for model fitting.

Engineered Features and Conventional Machine Learning

Engineered summaries impose a constraint on the information that a predictor can use. Measures of location, spread, amplitude, and trend can describe an operating state without requiring a high-capacity sequence model to be estimated from a small number of independent observations.

The literature is replete with evidence of compact representations. Hand-crafted local geometry is competitive in 3D inspection, and a hydraulic CNN paper reports 1.7% error for its best single sensor versus 20.5% error for all seventeen sensors (Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023; Goodarzi et al., 2023).

These results highlight the necessity for robust conventional references and careful selection of predictive information.

Industrial vision also offers lightweight alternatives. The weakly supervised inspection network uses a single camera, internal anomaly-aware features, and a compact CNN. Reported computational cost is 2.33 million parameters and 5.65 ms per image (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, Table V).

The study provides some relevant evidence on representation efficiency, but the anomalous training labels and modified MVTec supervision differ significantly from normal-only inspection.

Conventional and learned representations should be compared using the same prediction target, assessment observations, and appropriately fitted preprocessing, rather than ranked solely by model family.

Pretrained Visual Representations

Pretrained visual representations provide a strong scientific explanation for transferring knowledge learned from large image collections to industrial applications with far less supervision. The features they learn may well contain the local appearance, texture, and object configuration relevant to the downstream prediction task.

PatchCore successfully employs pretrained, locally aggregated intermediate CNN features together with a memory of nominal patches for industrial inspection (Roth et al., 2022, Section 3).

This provides evidence for investigating transferred spatial representations, though its nominal-only patch retrieval differs significantly from supervised action classification.

RT-DETR provides another architectural precedent in the form of its multiscale visual backbone (Y. Zhao et al., 2024, Sections 4.1–4.2). Features at several spatial scales would be viable candidates for the prediction target.

Its published detection accuracy and computational performance pertain to the complete detection architecture rather than the downstream classification performance of the extracted CNN features.

Asymmetric student-teacher and cross-modal mapping methods also provide further evidence for task-specific use of the learned visual representations (Rudolph et al., 2023; Costanzino et al., 2024).

Vision-language inspection introduces semantic information as another potential source of representation value. DSE-AD combines historical and current rail images with normal and abnormal position prompts and assesses pixel localisation on a further rail domain (Lei & Qi, 2025).

Its pair-reference and prompt assumptions differ significantly from those of isolated image classification.

Together, these studies point to pretrained visual features as plausible candidates, while also highlighting the need to assess their actual information content, trainable parameters, and observation horizon in the downstream monitoring task.

Temporal and Time-Series Representations

Temporal representation learning provides an opportunity to retain sequence information that would otherwise be lost in engineered summaries.

PatchTST addresses this through patch-based time-series representations and shared modelling across channels (Nie et al., 2023, Sections 3.1–3.2).

On the Weather dataset at a forecasting horizon of 96, the patch-plus-channel independent model obtains an MSE of .152, compared with .164 without patching (Nie et al., 2023, Table 7).

This provides positive evidence for patch-based representation learning in the forecasting setting under consideration. However, the relevance to industrial classification warrants separate investigation, as the prediction target, normalisation, supervision, and aggregation differ.

Video representation learning looks at another temporal structure. TimeSformer and Video Swin model the spatial and temporal relations between video frames (Bertasius et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2021).

These approaches provide an alternative to simply averaging independently encoded frames. The industrial CNN-LSTM remaining useful life study also shows the importance of observation length within its simulated prediction task (Al-Dulaimi et al., 2019).

The literature therefore motivates comparisons that explicitly account for sequence length, observation windows, temporal ordering, normalisation, pretraining, and fitting budget.

A Temporal Transformer and a compact statistical representation could well be preserving different aspects of the same sensor stream. Looking at their individual and combined performance helps assess whether the additional representation contains useful information for the task at hand.

Audio Representations

Acoustic representations include MFCCs, log-mel spectra, wavelet maps, GASF transforms and learned features.

The Welding Penetration Network uses VMD and GASF; DMAW uses Transformer representations adapted to the welding observations (Hua et al., 2026; Fan et al., 2026).

These are alternative forms of acoustic representation. An image-shaped acoustic representation is still one derived from sound rather than being an independently acquired camera stream.

The Audio Spectrogram Transformer is a pretrained candidate for acoustic representation learning (Y. Gong et al., 2021). The results on the general audio benchmark are sufficiently promising to warrant industrial investigation. However, the appropriateness of such representations will depend heavily on the particular acoustic environment and the specific prediction task.

MIMII and ToyADMOS both go some way to establishing the importance of background composition and noise environment (Purohit et al., 2019; Koizumi et al., 2019).

Together, these studies lead one to consider recording quality, normalisation, and consistent observation intervals when looking at audio representations.

Frozen Representations, Fine-Tuning, and Computational Restrictions

Freezing an encoder, fitting a small prediction head and then adapting the entire network all consume different amounts of statistical and computing resources.

Benchmark methods based on pretrained features show that you can get useful representations without going through the entire original representation-learning process on the industrial dataset (Roth et al., 2022; Costanzino et al., 2024).

The computational cost of pretraining is still part of the representation's provenance even if it's external to the downstream experiment.

Frozen feature extraction can therefore enable comparisons under a constrained fitting budget. Fine-tuning provides a very different opportunity in that it allows you to adapt the representations to the particular target observations. Which of the two is ultimately more effective can only be determined by empirical comparison, rather than by assuming one approach is in principle better than the other.

Distillation also makes a further distinction between training and deployment. The PGMTKD multimodal teachers are used to enrich a pressure-only student. The gated acoustic-vibration study reports a distilled LightGBM student, though the nature of the teacher features and the timing of the inputs remain ambiguous (C. Li et al., 2025; Huang & Tong, 2026).

Resource comparisons should therefore account for feature extraction, preprocessing, trainable components, and any teachers required during inference, rather than just the final prediction model.

These results motivate comparisons of representation and fitting procedures under plausible computing budgets.

Multimodal Fusion Strategies

Fusion strategies can be broken down according to where the information is fused. Early integration fuses raw observations or raw features. Intermediate integration fuses the learned representations. Prediction-level fusion fuses the outputs (e.g., class probability estimates, anomaly scores). Hybrid architectures use more than one level of integration.

These provide an operational taxonomy for comparing the studies reviewed. Adaptive reweighting refers to whether the combination varies according to the observation or its context and can therefore operate at different fusion levels.

Terminology from the source papers is kept where appropriate, and comparisons are based on the actual location and mechanism of information exchange.

Unimodal References and Incremental Modality Value

The strongest credible single-input predictor provides the reference for measuring the incremental value of fusion.

DMAW fuses the image and audio Transformer representations to classify the four welding states. The reported accuracy is 96.1% with fusion, 90.2% for the image model and 84.4% for the audio model (Fan et al., 2026, Table III).

This supports a predictive benefit from the assessed multimodal pipeline. The grouping of the assessment windows by welding run is still not resolved in the evaluation.

In contrast, the weld penetration study reports 100% clean test accuracy for its image-only, audio-only, and fused models (Hua et al., 2026, Tables 1–3).

The additional modality therefore provides no measurable gain in accuracy under that comparison.

A similar distinction is made in the case of robotic monitoring. The HANDOVER study reports 69.1% accuracy for video plus force/torque and 67.9% when gripper state is added to the I3D-D family (Thoduka et al., 2024b, Table III).

These are five-class outcome accuracies averaged over five training runs. Within that comparison, adding an observation reduced predictive performance.

The tool wear study reports an MAE of 6.6183 for their proposed model, 8.3002 for the visual ResNet50 and 60.5118 for the acoustic reference. All the other multimodal architectures evaluated have errors in excess of 45 (X. Zhang et al., 2026, Table 1).

The strong visual reference makes the improvement scientifically informative, though the architectural differences mean you cannot attribute all of it to fusion.

Transferable results on the difficulties of multimodal optimisation and on industrial all-channel losses underline the need to establish incremental gains against a strong admissible reference (W. Wang et al., 2020; Goodarzi et al., 2023).

Collectively, these studies support investigating the value of conditional fusion, rather than assuming that including additional modalities will necessarily improve prediction.

Feature Concatenation

Feature concatenation combines the representations with no explicit mechanism for adaptive allocation and is therefore a key reference point for any more sophisticated form of integration.

Effectiveness depends on feature scale, dimensionality, regularisation, and the quality of the constituent representations.

M3DM's direct concatenation ablation is worse than its point cloud reference, while the full hybrid system outperforms both individual inputs (Y. Wang et al., 2023, Table 4).

MADFlow also provides a further protocol-specific comparison between alignment and concatenation (Y. Li et al., 2025).

CFA provides transferable evidence from forecasting. For DLinear on the Energy dataset, the first concatenation gives an MSE of .670, compared with .263 without text and .243 with constrained fusion (S. Lee et al., 2026, Table 4).

The constrained fusion method also performs worse in a number of the evaluated scenarios, which again suggests that the benefits of an integration mechanism are highly task-dependent.

Manufacturing fusion can similarly improve one architecture at the expense of the other under the same broad prediction task (J. Li et al., 2022, Table 3).

These results establish concatenation as a valid comparator. It should be appropriately normalised and fitted, rather than implemented as an artificially weak baseline against which more complex methods are compared.

Prediction-Level and Late Fusion

Prediction-level fusion involves fusing the outputs of modality-specific predictors via techniques such as averaging, stacking, or evidence aggregation. This is a classification of where the fusion occurs; the individual predictors could be trained independently or as part of a wider training regime.

Thermal-vibration diagnosis provides direct evidence of physical camera-sensor prediction-level fusion. They use separate CNN softmax outputs with weighted Dempster-Shafer aggregation and report a clean 100% diagnostic rate, compared with 99.06% for IRT and 96.32% for vibration (Huo et al., 2022, Table VII).

The weights were fixed, and the sample-level cross-validation did not establish independence between physical runs.

HANDOVER compares late outputs with intermediate sensor integration in the RGB and optical flow branches (Thoduka et al., 2024b, pp. 5–7).

Cross-modal feature mapping and CPIR combine discrepancy maps, which need not be probabilities (Costanzino et al., 2024; Shangguan et al., 2025).

Some papers also use the term 'late fusion' to describe representation-level integration prior to classification (W. Wang et al., 2020). That underlines the importance of knowing what information you are actually fusing.

Stacked generalisation provides a scientific basis for learning a second-stage combination from cross-validated predictions. Probability outputs contain predictive information beyond the hard class labels so that a second-stage learner can estimate the joint utility of the outputs (Ting & Witten, 1999, Sections 2–3).

Ting and Witten fitted a linear least-squares combiner to the class probabilities. A test of such a combination needs predictions for observations held outside the fitting of its component models (Ting & Witten, 1999, Sections 2–3).

Gated and Context-Conditioned Fusion

Adaptive fusion is scientifically justified when the utility of the individual inputs varies from observation to observation.

Gated Multimodal Units do provide evidence of transferable conditional representation integration. The architecture fuses visual and textual information and achieves a macro F-score of .541, compared with .521 for the concatenation baseline in the movie-genre classification task (Arevalo et al., 2017, Section 3.1, Table 2).

The gate in that study operates on learned representations before classification.

Adaptive integration has also been looked at in an industrial context. DMAW achieved 96.1% accuracy, compared with 90.5% for summation and 92.6% for its gate comparator (Fan et al., 2026, Table V).

TC-MAF provides another approach whereby the auxiliary RGB evidence is bounded around a multimodal anchor using normal training dispersion and local disagreement (Deng et al., 2026, pp. 3–5).

These architectures illustrate various approaches to controlling information contribution.

An important evaluation dimension is what governs the allocation. GCCF-AD is described as dynamic fusion, but the formulation they report is a learned scalar with no explicit sample-quality input (An et al., 2026, p. 149226, Equation 8).

PGMTKD uses training losses to assign teacher contributions, and task loss balancing focuses on optimisation rather than any direct measure of input quality at deployment (C. Li et al., 2025; X. Zhang et al., 2026).

GAF-Net uses progressive feature feedback, and trustworthy fusion employs evidence-based aggregation (Huang & Tong, 2026; Ying et al., 2025).

The scientific distinction is what contributions can vary, what information determines that variation, and whether that variation improves predictive performance relative to a fixed combination.

Process-conditioned weighting provides an additional way to incorporate pertinent context, without requiring explicit physical law-constrained modelling.

Comparability and Fairness of Fusion Evaluations

A shared dataset name does not in itself constitute a matched experimental comparison.

Cohort construction, supervision, observation windows, pretraining, feature dimensionality, augmentation, and selection exposure could all contribute to differences between architectures.

The tool wear study alters the architectural components as part of the fusion, and the incomplete federated imagery study alters the client data access as well as the combining mechanism (X. Zhang et al., 2026; X. Zhao et al., 2026).

These are comparative assessments of fitted pipelines. Their interpretation differs from an experiment intended to isolate attention, modality contribution, or quality conditioning.

Comparisons using the same evidence sources can support a narrower attribution. TC-MAF retains the same evidence sources, comparing bounded fusion against static averaging, weighted scores, rank fusion, and a learned gate (Deng et al., 2026, Table 3).

On the flip side, the disagreement-aware study uses a locally released code baseline that performs worse than the published M3DM result (J. Xu et al., 2026, Table 4).

Distilled predictors can also access training information that is not available to standard unimodal models (C. Li et al., 2025).

These settings remain scientifically valuable, provided that the information and fitting conditions are clearly specified. Pipeline performance, incremental modality value, and the isolated effect of a fusion operator represent distinct evaluation questions.

Reliability and Robustness

The reliability of a multimodal architecture depends on both the prediction setting and the type of intervention acting on its inputs.

Missing channels, corrupted measurements, asynchronous observations and changes in operating conditions all represent different reliability issues. The evidence reviewed is therefore grouped by the particular condition under consideration rather than assigned to a single general robustness category.

Missing Modalities

Planned single-input deployment differs from an unannounced loss of an input in a model that normally expects complete observations.

In the initial appraisal, four of the 37 works examined planned missing-input inference and one examined simulated dropout (five of 37, or 13.5%, Table A.1). These counts describe different ways in which an input can be absent.

PGMTKD's pressure-only student and incomplete RGB-geometry methods do provide evidence for reduced input inference. ModDrop and the missing-modality Transformers add further transferable mechanisms and counterexamples (C. Li et al., 2025; Sui et al., 2026; Neverova et al., 2015; M. Ma et al., 2022).

The effectiveness of these mechanisms will, of course, depend on the training conditions and the specific missing-input patterns they have been assessed on.

TC-MAF explicitly evaluates unavailable 3D input. I-AUROC drops from .9788 to .9330 and P-AUPRO from .9901 to .9586 (Deng et al., 2026, Table 3).

This provides a quantified example of performance under a given missing modality condition.

Federated substation monitoring varies the proportion of incomplete clients to 80% (X. Zhao et al., 2026, Figure 8).

Its training and client-availability setting differs from an arbitrary inference-time camera failure.

ZSG-IAD reports input subset comparisons without fully defining the shared checkpoint and training exposure (Q. Chen et al., 2026). Missing training images in the hot rolling regime constitute another availability regime (L. Ma et al., 2025).

These studies collectively indicate the need to explicitly specify available modalities during both training and inference (i.e., availability masks), the policy for replacement or fallback, and the system's response when all required inputs are absent.

Noise and Partial Degradation

Physical monitoring studies do provide evidence of a number of controlled degradation mechanisms. Thermal-vibration diagnosis examines Gaussian noise and vibration bias/stuck faults, while welding penetration recognition examines artificial interference. Acoustic-thermal and acoustic-vibration studies look at other noise settings (Huo et al., 2022; Hua et al., 2026; Ying et al., 2025; Huang & Tong, 2026).

These interventions vary in the observations affected, the degree of corruption and the extent to which the model is exposed to degradation.

At -2 dB, noisy IR thermography gives 95.26%, which jumps to 97.6% when combined with fault-free vibration. Noisy vibration alone gives 36.86%, jumping to 94.2% when combined with fault-free IR thermography (Huo et al., 2022, Table VIII).

The comparison shows the value of a fault-free companion modality when another observation is synthetically corrupted.

The welding penetration study reports a fused accuracy of 93.33% under its interference condition (Hua et al., 2026, Table 3).

The corresponding corrupted unimodal comparisons are required in order to assess the incremental benefit of fusion in the interference condition. The reported intervention also leaves some uncertainty about the corruption operator, magnitude, and exposure matching.

Synthetic signal-to-noise interventions provide some controlled experimental evidence, but the conclusions are specific to the observations affected and the severities tested. Natural simultaneous degradation and ordinary defective objects are quite different phenomena.

These distinctions lead directly to a comparison of predictions from degraded input against those from static fusion and single-input predictors under the same reliability condition.

Temporal Misalignment and Distribution Shift

Temporal misalignment and distribution shift both affect multimodal prediction, albeit in different ways.

A clock offset can break correspondence between observations, whereas a distribution shift can change the conditions under which the learned representation or predictor is evaluated.

Registration studies have established the importance of alignment in multimodal monitoring. OmniFuser's repeated within-pass images provide a way to construct paired observations rather than directly test a synchronisation failure (Petrich et al., 2021; L. Chen et al., 2023; Z. Wang et al., 2025).

The literature also contains examples of evaluation under changed acquisition conditions. HANDOVER trains on one robot and evaluates on a different robot (Thoduka et al., 2024b, Table V), and there's a rail localisation study that evaluates a Subway-trained model on high-speed rail (Lei & Qi, 2025, Table III).

Target-adaptive methods, target-validated condition studies and independent build experiments are all different forms of access to the assessment domain (C. Wang et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2024; Huang & Tong, 2026; Bevans et al., 2023).

Rebuilding normal memories on Eyecandies using fixed fusion hyperparameters is evidence of recipe transfer, rather than deploying an unaltered model to an untouched target installation (Deng et al., 2026; J. Xu et al., 2026).

Architecture reuse, representation transfer, temporal offset tolerance, and unchanged-model generalisation each require distinct evaluation protocols.

Quality-Aware and Uncertainty-Aware Fusion

Quality-aware fusion is driven by the idea that the predictive utility of an input may vary with its quality. Signal quality, model confidence, calibrated probability and evidence consistency are all different beasts, though.

QMF furnishes a theoretical basis for studying sample-dependent weighting via generalisation upper bounds under stated relationships between uncertainty and loss, assuming a common training and test distribution (Q. Zhang et al., 2023, Sections 3.1–3.2).

The transferable UDML experiments train on controlled Gaussian corruption intensities and recognise that sample difficulty can be confused with modality corruption (Wei et al., 2026, pp. 4–5, 9).

These results lead us to ask whether the quality information we have available helps us identify when a particular observation is no longer useful.

QMF reports an NYU scene classification accuracy of 70.09% on clean inputs, compared to 70.30% for concatenation. At corruption intensity ten, it reports 55.60% compared to 51.99% for late fusion (Q. Zhang et al., 2023, Table 1).

These non-industrial RGB-depth comparisons do show a regime-specific advantage under corruption. The clean and corrupted comparisons quoted use different reference methods, so they should not be read as a clean-versus-corrupted trade-off against the same reference method.

The effect of the adaptation mechanisms also differs across prediction endpoints. The disagreement add-on increases the local MVTec image AUROC from .779 to .800 and, at the same time, reduces the Eyecandies image AUROC from .810 to .794, even though the pixel metrics have improved (J. Xu et al., 2026, Tables 4–5).

TC-MAF uses normal-training dispersion and local disagreement to control the contribution of auxiliary evidence (Deng et al., 2026).

Collectively, these findings establish a scientific basis for reducing reliance on a stream when the observable context is expected to indicate errors. They also underscore the need to assess the relationship between available quality indicators and actual predictive reliability.

QMF's theoretical justification does not validate the use of arbitrary quality proxies, and clean calibration can actually degrade after a distribution shift (Q. Zhang et al., 2023; Ovadia et al., 2019).

For quality-conditioned weighting, the relevant comparison is with a static model given the same training exposure to corruption. Performance on clean observations also matters.

Modality Dropout, Augmentation, and Fallback Mechanisms

Different robustness mechanisms deal with different aspects of input availability.

Training-time modality dropout exposes the model to certain absence patterns. Imputation produces substitute observations, and deterministic fallbacks route the prediction to an available unimodal component.

These approaches can generate similar outputs under certain conditions without relying on the same learned recovery mechanism.

Fuzzy regularisation works by introducing failures via partial corruption and by replacing the entire input with a constant or noise. The evaluation is of Gazebo pose regression using random sample splits of overlapping windows (Altinses & Schwung, 2023, pp. 3–5).

The investigation provides evidence for controlled input regularisation, with independent physical deployment outage performance remaining unaddressed.

OmniFuser describes dropout and masking without any quantified outage table (Z. Wang et al., 2025, p. 10).

Generated replacement observations can also have a negative impact on performance. Replacing the clean depth with CycleGAN output worsens the synthetic robot benchmark's MSE (from 2.1745 × 10^-3 to 13.472 × 10^-3) (Altinses & Schwung, 2025, Table 7).

These results motivate individual evaluation of learned recovery, training exposure, and explicit fallback policies. Static controls, with equivalent degradation exposure and predictions from the surviving input, provide a useful baseline for assessing adaptive robustness.

Evaluation Beyond Accuracy

The practical value of a monitoring architecture will depend entirely on how its predictions are evaluated, chosen, and interpreted.

Accuracy in prediction can be affected by class distribution, assessment grouping and decision thresholds. A single aggregate score therefore only provides one facet of the evidence needed to assess the performance of a multimodal industrial monitoring system.

The evaluation questions concern assessment independence, discrimination, calibration, uncertainty, interpretability, computational viability and reproducibility.

Grouped Evaluation and Leakage Prevention

The independent assessment unit may be a weld, a tool, a participant, a recording, a machine or a build.

Randomly partitioned observations can end up with shared acquisition contextual information or raw samples. The fuzzy-regularisation study forms 20-step windows with a stride of one and then randomly divides the resulting examples (Altinses & Schwung, 2023, pp. 4–5).

Its assessment therefore doesn't establish performance on unseen trajectories.

Weld run grouping is still unresolved in the closest welding studies (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026). These examples do show a potential dependence between the observations but do not establish any particular level of leakage.

The HANDOVER authors report an explicit participant-disjoint official split (Thoduka et al., 2024b).

Normalisation, feature selection and model parameter optimisation all require an appropriate split between model development and model assessment (Varma & Simon, 2006; Kapoor & Narayanan, 2023).

Out-of-fold stacking provides a mechanism for constructing second-stage prediction inputs without any in-sample predictions. Its validity also depends on how the underlying fitting, selection and calibration folds are constructed.

Repeatedly selecting follow-up experiments after looking at the assessment results introduces further selection bias, even if the individual model fits exclude their outer test labels.

These results motivate the development of assessment protocols that account for the physical origin and dependence of observations throughout the modelling process.

Discrimination, Calibration, and Decision Thresholds

Ranking, probability quality and thresholded decisions are three different aspects of a predictor.

AUROC measures discrimination across thresholds rather than performance at a single operational false alarm rate or the quality of the predicted probabilities.

MVTec AD shows that useful ranking can exist alongside fairly poor thresholded localisation. The strict FPR metrics highlight a different aspect of detection performance (Bergmann et al., 2019; Costanzino et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2025).

The rail vision-language study reports pixel-level metrics and optimal threshold F1 (Lei & Qi, 2025, p. 7). These metrics address a completely different evaluation problem than image-level alarm accuracy at a threshold chosen from the training or validation set.

In manufacturing, Petrich et al. (2021) report an accuracy of .9921 and a precision of .2487 on an imbalanced layer, which is a clear example of the impact of class distribution and threshold selection.

Validation-fitted calibration can improve in-distribution probability quality, while dataset shift can damage confidence estimates (Guo et al., 2017; Ovadia et al., 2019).

Proper scoring rules (negative log-likelihood, Brier score, etc.) provide a basis for assessing probability forecasts (Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). Their interpretation differs from that of discrimination or thresholded classification.

A generated confidence field or evidence mass cannot replace probability evaluation. Metrics should align with the prediction target and class distribution, and operational thresholds should be selected using admissible training or validation observations.

Uncertainty and Statistical Comparisons

Seed variation, acquisition group sampling and model selection uncertainty are all different sources of variation.

HANDOVER's reported standard deviations are for five training runs on a fixed test set, whereas a group bootstrap of saved predictions samples assessment units conditional on the fitted models (Thoduka et al., 2024b).

These approaches capture different facets of uncertainty and do not necessarily encompass all the variation expected from retraining or deployment in a different domain.

Cross-validation folds share training observations. Under the assumptions stated, the classic variance result shows that no universally unbiased estimator of cross-validation variance exists; ignoring covariance can substantially underestimate uncertainty (Bengio & Grandvalet, 2004, pp. 1096–1098, 1102–1104). Conditional uncertainty intervals can still be useful when the estimand and dependence assumptions are stated explicitly.

Small fusion effects, ambiguous repetition counts and conflicting descriptions of significance all warrant the same level of caution (Gu et al., 2024; J. Xu et al., 2025).

Paired performance differences on common assessment observations provide a much more direct comparison than unrelated score intervals. An interval which contains zero doesn't demonstrate equivalence, and a positive point estimate doesn't establish practical importance without an appropriate criterion.

Interpretability and Its Limits

Prediction scores, coefficients, gate traces and spatial maps can all tell you something regarding how the model is behaving.

The attention debate has both counterexamples to faithful explanation and evidence that the learned attention weights do, in fact, contain useful information. This leads to the conclusion that interpretability should be evaluated on a task-specific basis rather than drawing general conclusions from the terminology of the architecture itself (Wiegreffe & Pinter, 2019).

ZSG-IAD measures schema validity, language consistency and coarse-to-final-mask overlap (Q. Chen et al., 2026). These provide evidence of various forms of self-consistency.

Their interpretation differs from both independent physical defect causation and calibrated confidence.

HANDOVER leaves the use of human action prediction as a means of explanation as a future area of research (Thoduka et al., 2024b).

Describing which signals a model used leaves two further questions open: whether they explain the physical cause and whether the explanation helps an operator make a decision.

Process context should be distinguished from the explicit physical equation constraints (e.g., PGMTKD's pressure model; C. Li et al., 2025).

Modularity, Reproducibility, and Computational Cost

Modular architectures enable you to swap in and out individual representations and prediction components and compare them. Their value for scientific investigation comes from the fact that you can identify which component is different between conditions.

Modularity does not establish predictive robustness or an advantage over a matched end-to-end architecture.

An identified version, accessible source code, executable processing and independently reproduced results all provide various levels of reproducibility assurance (Pineau et al., 2021).

The difference between local and published M3DM results remains unresolved. It motivates checks of dependencies, valid point filtering, checkpoints, and scoring conventions (J. Xu et al., 2026).

Computational comparisons of course also require a common measurement boundary. The CPIR paper reports lower FPS than CFM, which is a clear performance vs speed trade-off (Shangguan et al., 2025).

The reported 5.65 ms for the lightweight image network doesn't include the independent sensor branch or acquisition delay (Z. Zhang et al., 2024, Table V).

TimeSformer's compute requirements depend on the ImageNet pretraining and the workload it is evaluated on (Bertasius et al., 2021).

Hardware, batch size, caching, synchronisation and the processing stages included all determine which computational measurements can be compared.

These results indicate the necessity for explicit resource accounting and reproducibility documentation. Prospective deployment and operator usability remain open questions for validating a successful offline forward pass.

Critical Synthesis and Research Gap

The reviewed literature shows that multimodal observations and learned representations can benefit industrial and robotic monitoring. Their value, however, depends on the prediction task, the information each representation contains, and the conditions under which the integration method is evaluated.

The previous sections have identified four related scientific questions: which representations are appropriate for a given target, whether combining their predictions is valuable, whether that value is maintained as observation quality degrades, and how the experimental assessments should be conducted.

Findings Supported Across the Reviewed Studies

There are tangible observations that can be used to improve the performance of a task-specific monitoring pipeline. DMAW, thermal-vibration diagnosis, HANDOVER and tool wear prediction are all good examples under their respective assessment protocols (Fan et al., 2026; Huo et al., 2022; Thoduka et al., 2024b; X. Zhang et al., 2026).

Controlled evidence integration can also improve RGB–geometry inspection under intact-input conditions. TC-MAF separately reports performance when the 3D input is unavailable (Deng et al., 2026).

The literature therefore establishes that useful industrial multimodal fusion mechanisms and approaches to dealing with incomplete observations exist.

Representation learning offers a number of scientifically sound options for extracting information relevant to the task. Pretrained convolutional features, patch-based temporal representations, and hand-crafted features are all candidates with some supporting evidence under certain learning and evaluation regimes (Roth et al., 2022; Nie et al., 2023; Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023).

Their suitability for another industrial prediction target depends on several factors, including the observations, the supervision, the aggregation, and the compute resources involved.

Static and learned fusion mechanisms both offer different avenues for exploiting complementary information. Stacked generalisation motivates learning combinations of the outputs of individual predictors. In contrast, gated representation learning and quality-aware fusion justify context-dependent allocation (Ting & Witten, 1999; Arevalo et al., 2017; Q. Zhang et al., 2023).

A further well-established distinction concerns input reliability. Planned single-input deployment, complete inference-time absence, controlled corruption, and target domain adaptation all address different issues (C. Li et al., 2025; Altinses & Schwung, 2023; X. Zhao et al., 2026).

The consistent finding across the reviewed studies is a conditional benefit under specific tasks and evaluation regimes, rather than universal superiority of any single representation or fusion architecture.

Unresolved Questions and Research Gap

The evidence reviewed also contains significant negative and conflicting findings.

The same clean-test accuracy is observed for the unimodal and fused welding models. HANDOVER performance drops when the gripper state is added. Some alternative multimodal tool-wear models perform worse than the visual reference, although the proposed model improves on it. The Eyecandies image scores also go down. All of this calls into question the notion of an unqualified fusion benefit (Hua et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b; X. Zhang et al., 2026; J. Xu et al., 2026).

Category-specific geometry losses, poor hydraulic channel combinations, and the difficulties of multimodal optimisation all reinforce this conclusion (Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023; Bonfiglioli et al., 2023; Goodarzi et al., 2023; W. Wang et al., 2020).

Several mechanisms could account for these results: redundancy, inappropriate representations, optimisation difficulties, noise, supervision and limited independent sample support. The studies do not point to a single underlying explanation.

An important unresolved issue is the distinction between a representation's standalone strength and its complementary predictive value. A representation that is weak in isolation may still provide information useful for another predictor. Conversely, a strong additional representation does not guarantee that a fusion mechanism can effectively utilise that information.

Reliability introduces a related source of uncertainty. Adaptive weighting is justified when the available context can identify changes in unimodal prediction reliability (Q. Zhang et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2026). The value of conditioning information, its relationship to branch errors, and its comparison with exposure-matched static alternatives must be investigated on a task-specific basis.

External evaluation adds another boundary. Re-using a procedure with a new dataset-specific fitting, re-using the learned representation weights and then evaluating an unaltered predictor in a different domain are all different experimental questions. You cannot just take the results of one and treat them as equivalent evidence of generalisation.

Final Summary of the Literature Review

The 94 appraised works support a conditional benefit from multimodal monitoring. In four-state welding classification, combining image and audio raised accuracy from 90.2% for the stronger single input to 96.1%. In weld-penetration classification, both individual inputs and their combination reached 100% on the clean test set (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026). In human–robot handover, adding gripper state to video and force/torque reduced five-class accuracy (Thoduka et al., 2024b). Taken together, these comparisons locate the architectural question more precisely: for a given target and observation window, when does a second input retain complementary information, and when does a learned fusion rule use it more effectively than a strong single predictor or a fixed combination under the same fitting and reliability conditions?

Table 2.1. Findings, limits and follow-up questions from the literature review

Adding an input

Published finding and its limit: Image and audio fusion gained 5.9 percentage points over image alone in four-state welding classification. In weld penetration, each single input tied fusion on the clean test. Adding gripper state reduced accuracy in handover (Fan et al., 2026; Hua et al., 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b). Each contrast applies to its own outcome, inputs and assessment cases.

Follow-up question: Does a second input improve on the strongest single input when both are tested on the same held-out runs?

Choice of fusion rule

Published finding and its limit: An RGB–3D inspection study compared a static mean with a learned gate using complete inputs (Deng et al., 2026). A gated unit outperformed concatenation on a movie-genre task. Some alternative multimodal tool-wear pipelines were weaker than the visual reference, although the proposed model improved on it (Arevalo et al., 2017; X. Zhang et al., 2026). Differences in encoders or fitting can change the ranking. The genre result has no direct industrial validation.

Follow-up question: Under which faults does a learned rule outperform a static rule after both receive the same exposure during training?

Training strategy

Published finding and its limit: Both models had 1.02% error on clean digits. With two quadrants missing, modality-dropout training reduced error from 35.91% to 7.19% (Neverova et al., 2015). This controlled digit comparison tests exposure to specified absence patterns, not performance under industrial outages.

Follow-up question: How much of a gain under corruption comes from training exposure, and how much from the fusion rule?

Corrupted inputs at test

Published finding and its limit: One bearing study compared fusion rules at 0 dB. Another reported the proposed model's accuracy when one input was degraded (Huang & Tong, 2026; Sun et al., 2026). Their corruption conditions and controls differ; the proposed model's corrupted accuracy alone does not establish incremental fusion benefit.

Follow-up question: At the same fault severity, does fusion outperform both the intact input and a static combination?

Input representation

Published finding and its limit: Hand-crafted geometry can support 3D inspection, temporal patches retain useful information for forecasting, and learned video features address action recognition (Horwitz & Hoshen, 2023; Liu et al., 2022; Nie et al., 2023). The tasks and supervision differ. These studies do not rank the representations on the same target.

Follow-up question: Which encoding adds complementary information when the target, observation window and fitting budget are fixed?

Missing inputs at inference

Published finding and its limit: A pressure-only student was trained for single-input deployment; modality-resilient inspection and RGB–geometry inspection also examine incomplete inputs (Deng et al., 2026; C. Li et al., 2025; J. Xu et al., 2025). A planned input restriction leaves performance under sudden loss or partial corruption open. Simulated dropout and inference-time absence require distinct assessment conditions.

Follow-up question: Does a policy trained for a known missing stream also cope with an unexpected outage during operation?

Transfer across settings

Published finding and its limit: Handover was tested across robots. A bearing study used target-domain validation during transfer (Huang & Tong, 2026; Thoduka et al., 2024b). Inspection methods were reapplied to Eyecandies with target-specific normal memories (Deng et al., 2026; J. Xu et al., 2026). Rebuilding those memories uses target data. Performance of an unchanged predictor at a new installation remains a separate question.

Follow-up question: How much fusion benefit persists on a new machine or site when access to target data is controlled?

How credible is the estimate?

Published finding and its limit: Participant-disjoint assessment tests performance on held-out people. Selection and calibration also affect what a reported score measures (Guo et al., 2017; Thoduka et al., 2024b; Varma & Simon, 2006). Independence depends on the physical grouping. Accuracy alone gives neither failure ranking nor probability quality.

Follow-up question: Does the apparent benefit persist with appropriate grouping, training-side selection and calibration, and assessment of ranking and probability quality?
